Anthropic, the company that develops the Claude artificial intelligence models, filed for an initial public offering with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) on 26 October. The filing is called an S-1. In it, a company discloses audited financial statements, risks and its governance before it sells shares to the public. The shares are to be listed on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. The filing does not yet include the share price or the number of shares to be sold.

Until now, all that was known about Anthropic came from company statements and media reports. Now the numbers are available. From April to June 2026 it had $11.6 billion of revenue, 14.7 times more than a year earlier. In the same three months it made money on its operations for the first time. At the same time, it has committed to paying partners hundreds of billions of dollars for chips, data centres and power over the coming years, mostly regardless of how much compute it actually uses.

This analysis therefore looks for the answer to one question: **can revenue grow fast enough for the compute commitments to become an advantage rather than a burden, and how much of that growth will the IPO price already include?**

## What Anthropic disclosed

Data for 2023 to 2025 is audited; this year's is not, which is normal for an S-1. The filing does not yet include results for July to September. First, a comparison of the first halves of the year:

| January to June | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $1.16bn | $16.29bn |
| Cost of compute | $2.33bn | $8.95bn |
| Operating result | −$2.14bn | −$0.69bn |
| Adjusted operating result | −$1.85bn | $1.62bn |
| Operating cash flow | −$1.51bn | $3.93bn |
| Net loss | −$12.97bn | −$80.75bn |

The **operating result** is the difference between revenue and the costs of the business itself, before interest, revaluations and taxes. The **adjusted operating result** is the company's own measure, not an accounting one: it excludes employee pay in shares, donations in shares and a one-off settlement over copyright. **Operating cash flow** shows how much cash the business actually brought in.

The table shows two pictures that seem to contradict each other. A business that last year lost almost two dollars on every dollar of revenue is close to break-even this year, and cash is coming in. Yet the net loss rose to $80.7 billion. Almost all of it is on paper, and no cash left the company because of it; the section on the loss explains why. First, though, where the growth comes from.

## Where the growth comes from

At most companies, growth fades as they get bigger. At Anthropic it has accelerated. Revenue rose from $41 million to $386 million in 2024 and to $4.6 billion in 2025. Compared with the previous quarter, revenue grew as follows:

- October to December 2025: +48%,
- January to March 2026: +132%,
- April to June 2026: +144%.

The company attributes the acceleration to new models. In November 2025 it released Opus 4.5, which could do more and was cheaper per completed task than its predecessor. When a task costs less, customers hand it to the model more often and find new uses for it. The growth resembles a multi-stage rocket: each new model is a new stage that ignites before the previous one burns out. Models released in the six months to the end of September 2026 brought in more than 80% of revenue from July to September.

The money comes mainly from businesses. Anthropic measures them by the number of customers that paid at least $100,000 over the last 12 months.

![The number of large customers grew more than 12-fold in a year](/images/articles/anthropic-s1-analysis-2026-10-30/kupci.svg)

These customers brought in 74% of revenue in the first half of the year. They include all ten of the largest US companies on the Fortune list. More than 1,900 customers pay at least $1 million a year, and about 250 at least $10 million. Existing customers keep spending more: **net revenue retention** (NRR), which shows how much the same group of customers pays today compared with a year ago, is about 900% according to the company. The same customers are therefore paying about nine times more than a year earlier.

Customers mostly pay for usage. A model cuts text into **tokens**, small units that it reads and writes, and the customer pays for the tokens used. In the first half of 2026, 80% of revenue came from usage and 20% from subscriptions. Almost half of sales, 48%, go through the cloud marketplaces of Amazon, Google and Microsoft. These supply compute and offer their own models, and some of them, Amazon and Google, are also Anthropic's investors.

The quality of this revenue has three limits that the filing itself states:

- **Policy violators.** The company estimates that 5% to 7% of revenue in the first nine months of the year came from customers who violate its usage policy. It stepped up enforcement in August and September, so this revenue will decline.
- **Seasonality.** Usage drops when people are on holiday. In the two weeks to 2 January 2026 it fell by about 20% compared with the two weeks before.
- **Concentration.** No customer accounts for more than 10% of revenue; the five largest together account for about 25%. The share of revenue from outside the top five rose from 64% in January to March to 79% in July to September.

The growth is therefore real and broad, but it rests on usage, which can change faster than subscriptions. Whether such growth turns into profit depends on the cost every AI developer faces: compute.

## Why the margin turned

**Compute** means the chips, servers, networks and power a model needs to be trained and to answer customers. Anthropic mostly secures it through multi-year contracts, 12 to 18 months before it receives it. The cost therefore rises when the capacity arrives, not when the customers do.

![Revenue has overtaken the cost of compute](/images/articles/anthropic-s1-analysis-2026-10-30/prihodki-racunska-moc.svg)

From April to June 2026, revenue was 14.7 times higher than a year earlier, the cost of compute 4 times. In January to March 2025, compute cost was 251% of revenue; in April to June 2026 only 48%. This is **operating leverage**: when costs are fixed in the short term, profit grows faster than revenue.

| Quarter | Operating margin | Adjusted margin |
|---|---|---|
| January to March 2025 | −249% | −221% |
| April to June 2025 | −152% | −129% |
| July to September 2025 | −224% | −93% |
| October to December 2025 | −139% | −84% |
| January to March 2026 | −35% | −13% |
| April to June 2026 | 8% | 19% |

The **margin** shows how much of every $100 of revenue is left as profit. In July to September 2025 the result was hit by a one-off settlement of a copyright class action (the Bartz case), $1.5 billion. The gap between the two columns in April to June 2026 is explained mostly by employee pay in shares, $1.26 billion. This is a real cost, because every new share reduces the stake of existing owners (this is called **dilution**). A more realistic picture of profitability is therefore closer to 8% than to 19%.

The company itself warns that the margin will fluctuate. When a large new block of compute arrives, the cost comes before the revenue that the capacity will enable. In the near term it will also buy more short-term compute, which is more expensive. The first profitable quarter is therefore not a new permanent level, but proof that the business can make money once revenue overtakes the capacity it has secured.

## Why an $80 billion loss if the business makes money

![Almost all of the loss is on paper, not in cash](/images/articles/anthropic-s1-analysis-2026-10-30/izguba-most.svg)

Most of the loss comes from **convertible notes**: a loan that converts into shares at the IPO. Anthropic issued $6.2 billion of them to Amazon. Because they will turn into shares, they are carried in the books at what those shares are worth. As the company's value jumped, their book value rose to $108.6 billion on 30 June 2026. The company booked the increase in the value of these and similar instruments as a $72.4 billion expense in the first half of the year. Another $8.8 billion from agreements with Google and Amazon to buy shares in the future works in a similar way.

It is like promising a friend part of your house: when the house rises in value, your obligation in the books grows, even though you have paid nothing. The better the company does, the larger this loss. At the IPO the notes convert into shares and the effect largely disappears. The accumulated loss since the company was founded is $132.9 billion.

Cash tells a different story. Operating cash flow from January to June was $3.9 billion, and capital expenditure $0.5 billion. **Free cash flow**, the cash left after capital expenditure, was therefore about $3.4 billion.

Two warnings for the future. First: employee pay in shares that the company has not yet booked amounts to $26.6 billion. A large part will be recognised in the quarter of the IPO, so the first report after the listing will show a large expense. It does not mean the business has worsened, but it does mean dilution. Second: positive cash flow is not permanent. The company expects that building its own compute will consume a lot of cash for the foreseeable future. How much cash it has and how much it has promised is therefore the core of the central question.

## How much cash it has and how much it has promised

Since it was founded, Anthropic has raised $141.1 billion of capital. At the end of June 2026 it had $131.7 billion of cash and short-term investments. Most of it was raised this year:

| Funding round (2026) | Raised | Price per share |
|---|---|---|
| Series G (January, February) | $30.2bn | $25.91 |
| Series G-2, Google and Amazon (April to June) | $15.0bn | $25.91 |
| Series H (May, June) | $65.2bn | $58.90 |

The prices are adjusted for the 10-for-1 stock split the company carried out in September 2026. The price per share rose 2.3-fold in four months. Google can invest up to another $20 billion by 2030, but its stake is capped at 14.99%.

There is a lot of cash, but even more commitments.

![Compute commitments with eight partners](/images/articles/anthropic-s1-analysis-2026-10-30/zaveze.svg)

At the end of 2025, Anthropic had $54.6 billion of non-cancelable purchase commitments. By 30 June 2026 they had grown to about $265.6 billion. That is what the company would earn in almost six years at its April to June pace. After 30 June it signed new contracts and amended existing ones, with minimum payments of about $245 billion long-term and $21 billion short-term. The filing does not yet state the total as of 30 September.

The contracts differ. With Google and Amazon, Anthropic must pay the difference even if it uses less compute. With SpaceXAI it will pay about $3.1 billion a month once all the capacity is delivered in December 2026. After a non-cancelable period ending in January or March 2027, the agreements can be cancelled with 90 days' notice. With Broadcom, it is a five-year lease of equipment with TPU chips in data centres operated by others on its behalf.

The company calls this last approach Anthropic Direct. It secures data centre space early, because it takes the longest to get, and picks the expensive chips later, when it knows better how many it needs. It is similar to an airline that orders aircraft years in advance. If the passengers come, it earns a lot. If they don't, it still has to pay for the aircraft.

The link with Google is particularly close. Google is at once Anthropic's cloud and chip supplier, an investor, and a competitor with its Gemini models. What this means for Google is covered in our [analysis of Alphabet after the third quarter](/en/analysis/alphabet-analysis-2026-10-29/).

## Management and governance

A buyer of shares on the stock market will get a share of the profits, but not control of the company. Anthropic is incorporated as a **public benefit corporation** (PBC) under the law of the US state of Delaware. Its board must therefore balance three things: the financial interests of shareholders, the interests of those affected by the company, and the public benefit written into its charter, which is the responsible development of advanced AI.

Control is divided as follows:

- The seven co-founders will hold 50.1% of the votes at shareholder meetings through a special entity (Class F).
- An independent trust, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, with five trustees who have no financial stake, elects and removes a majority of the board. Because of this, the company is a controlled company under Nasdaq rules; it will use the exemption from the independence rules only for its nominating committee.
- A public benefit fund owned by the company received 846.8 million shares, 5% of all shares. At the Series H price they are worth about $50 billion. Each time the share price rises by 50%, it can receive up to 2% more shares.
- Each co-founder has pledged to give 80% of their stake to charity.

For a long-term investor this means two things. Management can take long-term decisions without stock market pressure, including ones that reduce profit for the sake of safety. Shareholders, however, cannot outvote such decisions. The **lock-up** also matters: the period during which existing owners may not sell shares. For the co-founders and larger owners it ends after the results for July to September 2027 are published or after 365 days, for the others after the results for January to March 2027 or after 180 days, whichever comes first. At that point a lot of shares can come onto the market.

These facts can lead to two conclusions about the future.

## The bear case: the commitments stay, the lead is short

The bear case is the view of those who expect harder times. For Anthropic it runs like this:

1. The commitments run into hundreds of billions of dollars and are mostly non-cancelable. They will have to be paid regardless of revenue.
2. Revenue rests on usage, which can fall faster than subscriptions. Part of it will drop out because of policy violators.
3. The lead is short. More than 80% of revenue comes from models less than six months old, and customers move quickly to a newer model. If a competitor such as OpenAI or Google releases a better model, or an open model that anyone can use for free becomes good enough, Anthropic can quickly lose share.
4. If growth stalls, the commitments remain, the margin falls, and the company will need new money, probably by issuing shares.

On top of this come risks the company does not control. In February 2026 the US President told federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's technology, and the Department of War designated it a supply-chain risk. The trigger was the company's stated position that its technology should not be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons. In August a district court ruled largely in Anthropic's favour. In September, however, an appeals court upheld one of the designations by two votes to one, and the proceedings continue. Government contracts directly bring in about 1% of revenue, but the designation could deter companies that do business with the US government. In June 2026 the Department of Commerce restricted exports of the Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models, which the company switched off for all customers until the restrictions were lifted.

Add to that dilution ($26.6 billion of unbooked share-based pay and extra shares for the fund), dependence on clouds that are also competitors, and copyright disputes that did not end with the Bartz settlement: music publishers and Reddit, among others, are suing it. The outcome would be a company with large fixed obligations and revenue that depends on each next model being the best.

## The bull case: demand outpaces compute, customers keep spending more

The bulls do not deny that the commitments are large. But they argue that they are a source of advantage.

**Customers are not just staying, they are spending more and more.** Net retention of about 900% and 12 times more large customers in a year are not a sign of fickle usage, but of spreading within businesses. The share of revenue from outside the five largest customers is growing, which reduces dependence on individual customers.

**Operating leverage works.** The cost of compute fell from 251% to 48% of revenue, the company made money on its operations for the first time and generated free cash flow. This is proof that the business model holds once the chips are secured.

**Cheaper models increase usage.** When Opus 4.5 made tasks cheaper, quarter-on-quarter growth rose from 48% to 132%. In September 2026 Opus 5.5 was released, which the company says is again cheaper per task. When something becomes cheaper, total usage can rise by more than the price fell.

**The company is efficient.** It has about 5,300 employees. Revenue per employee exceeded $6.6 million over the last 12 months, compared with $1.4 million a year earlier. More than 90% of contributions to the company's code in September 2026 were written by Claude.

**The commitments secure access to the bottleneck.** According to the company, demand for compute has long exceeded the capacity available to it. Whoever has it secured across four chip families (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Nvidia and AMD) can serve demand when others cannot. With $131.7 billion of cash, it also has a cushion for weaker quarters.

Who is right depends on how much growth the buyer of a share will pay for.

## What the price already includes

There is no IPO price yet, and no analysts who cover the company regularly. There are therefore two reference points. The first is the Series H price: $58.90 per share multiplied by about 16.9 billion total shares (derived from the fact that 846.8 million shares are 5%). That gives about $1,000 billion. The second is media reports that the company is aiming for $1,500 to $2,000 billion. This is not company data.

The simplest measure is the **revenue multiple**: the value of the company divided by annual revenue. For annual revenue we take the April to June pace multiplied by four ($46.2 billion).

| Company value | Price per share | Multiple of annual pace | Multiple at $100bn revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1,000bn | $59.2 | 21.6 times | 10 times |
| $1,500bn | $88.8 | 32.5 times | 15 times |
| $2,000bn | $118.3 | 43.3 times | 20 times |

After the IPO there will be more shares (new shares, employee pay), so at the same company value the price will be somewhat lower. The multiple does not say what the commitments cost or how fast revenue will grow.

A **reverse DCF** shows that. Instead of forecasting the future and calculating a value, we take the value and calculate what revenue growth it already includes. The model accounts for the fact that growth costs money, because part of the profit has to go into investment. The starting point is revenue of $46.2 billion and a margin of 8.4% from April to June. The **required return** is the return an investor wants per year; for a young company it is higher than for a mature one.

| Company value | Margin in five years 25% | Margin in five years 35% |
|---|---|---|
| $1,000bn | 48.7% | 41.1% |
| $2,000bn | 66.8% | 58.6% |

The table shows annual revenue growth over the next five years at a 10% required return. After five years, growth gradually falls to 2.5%. At a 12% required return and a 35% margin, 50.2% would be needed at a value of $1,000 billion and 69.0% at $2,000 billion.

![What revenue the valuation implies](/images/articles/anthropic-s1-analysis-2026-10-30/obratni-dcf.svg)

At $1,000 billion, Anthropic would need about $258 billion of annual revenue in five years. At $2,000 billion, about $464 billion, almost as much as Alphabet, Google's parent company, has today. Growth of about 40% a year is less than Anthropic achieves now. For comparison: Alphabet's revenue grew by 11.8% a year from 2021 to 2025.

What an investor gets at both values if one of three paths plays out (an illustration, not a forecast):

| Path over the next five years | Revenue 2031 | Return at $1,000bn | Return at $2,000bn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slowdown: growth 30%, margin 25% | $172bn | 6.5% | 4.6% |
| Central: growth 45%, margin 30% | $296bn | 10.1% | 7.0% |
| Favourable: growth 60%, margin 35% | $484bn | 14.1% | 10.3% |

This gives the **asymmetry**, the difference between how much you can lose and how much you can gain. At $1,000 billion the central path delivers about 10% a year; at $2,000 billion only 7%. On the downside: if growth falls to 30% and the margin stays below 25%, the return is low. In a worse outcome, where the commitments remain and new shares have to be issued, it can be negative. On the upside: if agents spread into most office work and the company stays at the frontier, revenue can exceed even the favourable path. The higher the IPO price, the more has to go right.

## What to watch to know who is right

All six points are different views of the same question: is revenue growing faster than the compute commitments?

1. **Price range and number of shares.** They will come in an amendment to the filing (S-1/A). The value can then be put into the table above.
2. **Revenue for July to September 2026 and the annual revenue pace.** Both fields are still blank in the filing.
3. **The margin when new compute arrives.** At SpaceXAI the cost will be about $3.1 billion a month. If the margin falls because of new capacity and then rises again, operating leverage works.
4. **Quality of revenue.** The share from policy violators, the number of customers above $100,000, and net retention.
5. **The dispute with the government and export restrictions.** The decision on a rehearing before the appeals court.
6. **Dilution.** The cost of share-based pay in the quarter of the IPO and the end of the lock-up in 2027.

## A conclusion everyone has to draw for themselves

The S-1 is the first audited look at the finances of a leading AI developer, and it shows two sides. The business works: revenue rose almost 15-fold in a year, customers keep spending more, revenue has overtaken the cost of compute and the company made money on its operations for the first time. At the same time it has committed to hundreds of billions of dollars of compute, and its revenue rests on usage and on each next model being the best.

Those who believe demand for AI is only beginning see a company that secured the industry's bottleneck in advance. Those who doubt it see obligations that remain even if growth stalls, and shareholders without a say in decisions. Who is right will be shown by the ratio between revenue and the cost of compute in the coming quarters. How much of that future a buyer will already be paying for will be shown by a price that does not exist yet.
