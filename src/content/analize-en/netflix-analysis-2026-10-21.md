Netflix reported its third-quarter results on 20 October, after the US market closed. Profit beat the forecast, but the forecast for the fourth quarter came in below analysts' expectations. Even before the results, the stock was 44.7% below its 12-month peak.

In its shareholder letter the company wrote for the first time that it is aware of investors' concern about growth in viewing hours. That concern is in the title of this analysis: the money Netflix earns is growing much faster than the time people spend with its content. The analysis therefore follows one question: **how long can profit keep growing faster than viewing, and how much of that growth is already in the price?**

## What Netflix said

The quarter itself delivered on the forecast. Revenue landed on the forecast, margin and profit above it:

| Third quarter | A year earlier | Company forecast (16 Jul) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $11.51bn | $12.86bn | $12.87bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.2% | +11.7% | +11.8% |
| Operating margin | 28.2% | 33.2% | **34.6%** |
| Earnings per share | $0.59 | $0.82 | **$0.87** |

**Operating income** is profit from the business itself, before interest and taxes. **Operating margin** shows what share of revenue remains as operating income. The company attributes the higher-than-forecast margin mainly to the timing of expenses: some costs come later than planned.

For an investor, what the company said about the future matters more:

| Company forecast of 20 Oct | Forecast | For comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 revenue | $13.44bn (+11.5%) | analysts: $13.51bn |
| Q4 operating margin | 26.0% (27.2% excluding layoff costs) | last year: 24.5% |
| 2026 advertising revenue | about $2.7bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $3bn |
| 2026 free cash flow | about $12.0bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $12.5bn; lower because of a real estate transaction in Q4 |

The revenue forecast for the fourth quarter is only half a percent below expectations. So why does it matter? Because of advertising. The company cut its advertising forecast by a tenth, $300 million, and did so in October, with little more than a quarter of the year left. Advertising is meant to be Netflix's next source of growth. This year it will bring in a little over 5% of revenue and, according to co-CEO Greg Peters, it is growing by more than 80%, but it is behind target because the company is rolling out some advertising capabilities more slowly.

Profit, however, is still forecast to grow faster than revenue. Operating income is expected to be about 18% higher than a year earlier in the fourth quarter, on revenue 11.5% higher. How that is possible is the core of this analysis.

A week before the results, management announced it would lay off about 6% of staff, the first time since 2022, and named a new content chief, **Peter Rice**, formerly president of Disney's television business.

## Where growth comes from

Netflix has three sources of growth: new memberships, higher prices and advertising. Advertising is still small, so most revenue growth comes from the first two. They show how growth has changed.

The biggest market is the US and Canada. At the end of March 2026 Netflix raised the price of all its US plans:

| US plan | Monthly price before | From the end of March 2026 | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard with ads | $7.99 | $8.99 | +12.5% |
| Standard | $17.99 | $19.99 | +11% |
| Premium | $24.99 | $26.99 | +8% |

![US and Canada: growth is slightly higher again after the price increase](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/rast-zda-kanada.svg)

Revenue in the US and Canada grew 11% in the third quarter, the first with the full effect of the price increase. Since prices rose by 8 to 12.5%, most of that growth can already be explained by higher prices and advertising, leaving little for new memberships. This cannot be checked directly, because Netflix no longer publishes membership numbers.

![Most growth comes from outside the US and Canada](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/regije-delezi.svg)

Outside the US and Canada, where Netflix generates 56% of its revenue and 59% of its growth, growth is easing: excluding currency effects, it slowed in the third quarter in all three regions.

New memberships are linked to viewing, and this is where the tension the company acknowledged lies. Viewing hours grew 2% in the first half of 2026 and 1.5% last year. A simple example shows why this matters: if memberships grew 5% and viewing hours only 2%, each member would be watching less. If members are watching as much as before, membership is barely growing. Which explanation holds is the core of the dispute between bears and bulls below.

Growth is therefore increasingly carried by prices and advertising, less by new memberships. Even so, profit is growing faster than revenue. The next section explains why.

## Why profit grows faster than revenue

Netflix sells a monthly subscription to series, films, live events and games, increasingly with advertising. At the end of 2025 it had more than 325 million paid memberships.

The whole business rests on one property: **a series costs the same whether a hundred million households watch it or three hundred million.** The cost of content is fixed in advance, while revenue grows with every new membership and every price increase. In the reports this cost is called **content amortization**, because Netflix spreads the payment for a film or series over the years in which the content is watched.

![Content cost takes a falling share of revenue, operating margin is rising](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/vsebine-in-marza.svg)

| Item | 2021 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $29.7bn | $45.2bn | +52% |
| Content cost | $12.2bn | $16.4bn | +34% |
| Operating income | $6.2bn | $13.3bn | **+115%** |
| Content cost as a share of revenue | 41.2% | 36.3% | −4.8 points |
| Operating margin | 20.9% | 29.5% | +8.6 points |

Over these four years content cost grew more slowly than revenue, so of every additional dollar of revenue, 46 cents remained as operating income. This is how profit can grow faster than viewing: as long as people stay subscribed and accept higher prices, they do not have to watch more. The mechanism also works in reverse. In 2022 content cost grew 14.7% and revenue only 6.5%, and the margin fell to 17.8%.

The mechanism therefore has one condition: revenue has to grow faster than content cost. In 2024 and 2025 the gap was about eight percentage points. This year the company expects revenue growth of 13% and content cost growth of about 10%, so **the gap narrows to about three points.**

![The gap that margin comes from is narrowing this year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/skarje-rasti.svg)

This already shows in the margin. On a comparable basis, excluding last year's one-off charge in Brazil, it is widening ever more slowly:

| Comparable basis | Last year | This year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating margin, first nine months | 33.2% | 33.5% | +0.3 points |
| Operating margin, full year (2026 forecast) | 30.9% | 31.5% | **+0.6 points** |
| Operating income growth, full year | | about 15% | the company cites more than 20% |

The company forecasts operating income growth of more than 20% this year, but last year's base was reduced by a one-off charge (all one-off effects are collected in the next section). The comparable column is an Engineering Investor calculation. The mechanism still works, then, but with an ever smaller cushion. If revenue growth slips below content cost growth, the margin stops widening and profit can no longer grow faster than the business.

## How much cash remains

Profit in the accounts is not yet cash. **Free cash flow** is cash from operations minus investment in equipment, and at Netflix it shows something the margin does not.

| First nine months | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content cost in the accounts | $11.66bn | $12.76bn | +9.5% |
| Investment in new content (cash) | $12.04bn | $14.93bn | **+24%** |
| Free cash flow excluding the termination fee | $7.6bn | $7.2bn | lower |

Netflix invested 24% more in new content than last year and considerably more than it expensed. The difference will reach the expense line in the coming years, but it already shows in cash: despite 15% growth in operating income, free cash flow is lower than last year.

![This year more goes into buybacks than there is free cash](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/denar-in-odkupi.svg)

Netflix spends almost all of its free cash flow on **share buybacks**: it buys its own shares on the market and retires them, so each remaining share represents a larger part of the company. This year it has spent more on buybacks than it generated in free cash and covered the difference from its cash. In the third quarter it bought back 63.9 million shares at an average price of about $75 (Engineering Investor calculation). Authorised buybacks of $22.3 billion remain, 7.7% of market value. Stock-based compensation, which free cash flow does not deduct, amounted to $405 million over nine months, about 4% of free cash flow.

Three one-off effects distort this year's figures. They are collected in one place so that readers do not have to look for them in the text:

| One-off effect | Amount | What it affects | In this analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax dispute in Brazil (Q3 2025) | charge of $619m | last year's operating income and margin | last year's base excludes it |
| Termination fee from withdrawing from the Warner Bros. deal (Q1 2026) | receipt of $2.8bn, about $2.26bn after tax | net income (about $0.53 per share) and cash flow | deducted |
| Real estate transaction (Q4 2026) | outlay of about $0.5bn | free cash flow | kept, as it is an actual outlay |

Netflix received the termination fee in February 2026, when it walked away from buying the film and streaming business of Warner Bros. Discovery after Paramount Skydance offered more. Excluding the fee, this year's free cash flow is about **$9.7 billion**, and about $10.2 billion without the real estate transaction.

Three more numbers describe the business's returns and risks:

- **Return on invested capital (ROIC)** is about **38%**: every dollar of equity and borrowed capital in the business generates about 38 cents of after-tax profit. The better-known return on equity (ROE) is less useful here, because buybacks and the termination fee inflate it artificially.
- **Debt is small.** Net debt of $7.1 billion is a little under half of one year's operating income.
- **Content is committed in advance.** Contractual content obligations amount to **$25.2 billion**, half of annual revenue. This is the other side of the mechanism: the cost is fixed in advance even if viewers leave.

The business is profitable and carries little debt, but it has to put ever more cash into content. What remains open is the question from the introduction: whether viewing will hold up long enough for the mechanism to keep working. This is where opinions part.

## The bear case: viewers are going elsewhere

The bear case starts with viewers' time. Netflix competes for it with everyone at once, and among its rivals it lists Alphabet (owner of YouTube), Amazon and TikTok. For Alphabet and Amazon, video is only part of a broader business. Nielsen measures what share of their TV time Americans spend on each service:

![Netflix is second in the US, but lost a point in a year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/gledanost-zda.svg)

| Share of TV time in the US (Nielsen) | July 2025 | July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | 13.4% | 14.2% (record) | +0.8 points |
| Netflix | 8.8% (record) | **7.8%** | **−1.0 points** |

The comparison is somewhat unfair to Netflix. According to Nielsen, July 2025 was its record month, with Squid Game as the most watched title, while July 2026 was marked by the end of the football World Cup. A drop of a full point therefore probably overstates the trend. That YouTube set a record despite the World Cup remains a strong argument.

Netflix's own data points the same way. In 2024 the company cited about two hours of viewing per membership per day; in the first half of 2026 it was about 1.6 (Engineering Investor calculation). On this data, two banks downgraded the stock in September: Wells Fargo to a rating equivalent to "sell", with a price target of $57, and HSBC to "hold", with a price target of $76.

Since growth in the US is carried mainly by price, the bears' question becomes: how much further can prices rise before people start cancelling? The logic of the case runs through the same mechanism as above, only in reverse:

1. Less viewing means a membership is worth less to a household.
2. A membership worth less is harder to raise in price and easier to cancel.
3. Revenue growth slows, while content cost is fixed in advance.
4. A fix costs money: more original content, more licensed content and sport, or an acquisition.

The outcome would be a repeat of 2022, when cost grew faster than revenue and the margin fell.

The bears are also helped by the fact that their case will become harder to test. Netflix no longer publishes membership numbers, and from 2027 the viewing report will come out once a year instead of twice.

A deeper reason for concern lies in costs. Most content on YouTube is created by users themselves, so YouTube does not carry the risk that a title will fail. Netflix carries it in full.

## The bull case: the frightening numbers have another explanation

The bulls do not deny that viewing is growing slowly. They argue that the business is not suffering from it, and they have some evidence for this in the reports.

**Members are not leaving, even though they pay more.** According to the company, the share of members who cancel improved in the third quarter globally and in the US, despite the March price increase. Revenue growth in the US and Canada even accelerated after it, from 10% to 11%. If a membership really were worth less to people, they would cancel more often at a higher price.

**Hours per membership fell because there are more memberships.** This answers the simple example above. When Netflix began charging for password sharing in May 2023, people who had watched on someone else's account opened their own. There were 260 million memberships at the end of 2023 and 302 million at the end of 2024. Their viewing had already been counted in total hours, so the same number of hours was spread across more memberships.

![Many more memberships, only slightly more viewing hours](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/narocnine-in-ure.svg)

**Nielsen measures one screen in one country,** while Netflix generates more than half of its revenue elsewhere. In India, with a strong slate of local content, viewing hours grew 24% in the third quarter and revenue excluding currency effects 18%.

**Not all hours are worth the same.** This year live events will take a little over 5% of content spending and bring only about 1% of viewing hours. A live hour is therefore expensive, yet six of the ten days with the most new members in the past five years were linked to live events. An hour that brings in a new member is worth more than an hour that merely fills an existing member's evening.

**The slowdown is mainly the comparison base.** Growth over the past two years was driven by paid password sharing in 2024 and the price increases at the start of 2025, after which growth in the US and Canada jumped to 18% (see the US and Canada growth chart above). This year Netflix is being compared with exactly those quarters.

**Rivals are indebted.** Paramount issued $52 billion of debt to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery, and according to the Financial Times the combined company will have about $80 billion of debt. Such a rival will find it harder to increase content spending and has a reason to sell its titles to others. That this is more than theory is shown by Disney, which has already started licensing its films and series to Netflix.

## What analysts think

Most analysts remain on the bull side, but with lower targets after the results:

| Analysts | Value | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Number of analysts (S&P Global) | **52**, consensus "buy" | Most remain on the bull side |
| Average price target | $91.39 | 33% above the price of $68.69 |
| Range of targets | from **$57** to **$126** | Disagreement by more than a factor of 2 |

After the results, price targets were cut by BofA (from $105 to $90), UBS (from $115 to $100) and Deutsche Bank (from $95 to $93), among others. Morningstar, which does not set a price target but estimates fair value, kept its estimate at $80 and still expects double-digit annual profit growth.

## What the price already includes

![Netflix: share price over the last 12 months](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/cena-12-mesecev.svg)

All ratios in this analysis are based on the last close before the results, **$68.69** (20 Oct 2026). The analysis is written for long-term investors, so it does not follow the swings in the days after the results.

Once the termination fee is deducted, the stock is more expensive than it first appears:

| Ratio at a price of $68.69 | As reported by the company | Excluding the fee |
|---|---|---|
| Price/earnings, last 12 months | 19.9 ($3.46) | **23.4** ($2.93) |
| Price/earnings, 2026 (nine months and the Q4 forecast) | 19.2 ($3.57) | **22.6** ($3.04) |
| Price/earnings, analyst estimate for 2027 | 18.1 ($3.80) | 18.1 ($3.80) |
| Market value/free cash flow 2026 | 24 ($12.0bn) | **28 to 30** |

The fee thus makes past profit look higher and future growth lower. The 2027 earnings estimate is about 6% above this year's earnings as reported by the company, and **about 25%** above this year's earnings excluding the fee.

A more useful question than whether the stock is "cheap" is the one from the introduction, translated into numbers: **what earnings growth does Netflix need for a purchase at $68.69 to deliver a decent return?**

![What has to hold for a purchase at this price to work out](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/scenariji-donosa.svg)

The starting point is this year's earnings per share excluding the termination fee, about $3.04. The table shows the annual return over the next five years for different earnings growth rates and different price/earnings ratios the market would pay in five years. Three examples:

- **8% growth and a ratio of 15** → −0.5% a year.
- **12% growth and a ratio of 20** → 9.3% a year.
- **16% growth and a ratio of 25** → 18.4% a year.

What is realistic? Analysts expect revenue growth of 11.2% next year. Even 10% revenue growth at an unchanged margin, together with buybacks, would mean earnings per share growth of about 13 to 14%. For more than that, the margin has to keep widening, which means revenue has to keep outpacing content cost. We are back at the question from the introduction.

The market has already cut the multiple sharply over one year. According to Deutsche Bank, in June 2025 the stock traded at about 40 times expected earnings; today it is about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings. The price therefore already assumes slower growth. It does not, however, protect against a shrinking margin: if earnings grow less than 8% a year and the market pays 15 times for them, the return is about zero.

One more number helps compare Netflix with other investments. Free cash flow excluding the termination fee and after stock-based compensation amounts to about 3.2% of market value: that is what an owner would receive each year if the company paid out all its cash and stopped growing.

## What to watch to know who is right

All four points are different views of the same question: can profit keep growing faster than viewing?

1. **The gap between revenue growth and content cost growth.** About three points this year. If it closes, the margin stops widening.
2. **Revenue growth in the US and Canada.** +11% in the third quarter, mostly because of the price increase. Once its effect fades from the second quarter of 2027, growth will show how much new memberships and advertising contribute.
3. **Content investment relative to the expense in the accounts.** The ratio was 1.17 over nine months. A higher ratio without faster growth means that keeping viewers is getting more expensive.
4. **Viewing hours and the first content under the new chief.** Netflix's next viewing report comes in early 2027, while series and films commissioned by Peter Rice will take a year or two.

The next milestone is the fourth-quarter results in January 2027, together with the first forecast for 2027.

## A conclusion everyone has to draw for themselves

The third quarter confirmed both stories at once. The business works: revenue is growing at double digits in every region, the margin is still widening on a comparable basis, and buybacks are at a record. At the same time, growth is increasingly carried by prices, the company itself has admitted that **viewing is not growing as fast as the money**, and free cash flow excluding the termination fee is lower than last year over nine months.

Anyone who thinks slow viewing is noise today pays about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings for a business with a 38% return on capital. Anyone who thinks it is an early sign sees $25 billion of content committed in advance, prices that cannot rise forever and a margin that is widening by only a little over half a point a year. Who is right will be shown by the gap between revenue growth and content cost growth, the very number through which Netflix doubled its profit over the past four years.

Margin and operating income growth "excluding the Brazilian charge" are Engineering Investor calculations, not company figures. The 2026 content share in the chart is calculated from the company's forecast. Nielsen's shares apply to TV screens in the US and do not cover viewing on phones or in other countries. The return scenarios illustrate the arithmetic and are not a forecast.
