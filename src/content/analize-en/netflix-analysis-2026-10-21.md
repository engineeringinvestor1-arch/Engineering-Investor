Netflix reported its third-quarter results on 20 October, after the US market closed. Profit beat the forecast, but the forecast for the fourth quarter came in below analysts' expectations. Even before the results, the stock was 44.7% below its 12-month peak.

In its shareholder letter the company wrote for the first time that it is aware of investors' concern about growth in viewing hours. That concern is in the title of this analysis: the money Netflix earns is growing much faster than the time people spend with its content. The analysis therefore follows one question: **how long can profit keep growing faster than viewing, and how much of that growth is already in the price?**

## What Netflix said

The quarter itself delivered on the forecast. Revenue landed on the forecast, margin and profit above it:

| Third quarter | A year earlier | Company forecast (16 Jul) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $11.51bn | $12.86bn | $12.87bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.2% | +11.7% | +11.8% |
| Operating margin | 28.2% | 33.2% | **34.6%** |
| Earnings per share | $0.59 | $0.82 | **$0.87** |

**Operating income** is profit from the business itself, before interest and taxes. **Operating margin** shows what share of revenue remains as operating income. The company attributes the higher-than-forecast margin mainly to the timing of expenses.

For an investor, what the company said about the future matters more:

| Company forecast of 20 Oct | Forecast | For comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 revenue | $13.44bn (+11.5%) | analysts: $13.51bn |
| Q4 operating margin | 26.0% (27.2% excluding layoff costs) | last year: 24.5% |
| 2026 advertising revenue | about $2.7bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $3bn |
| 2026 free cash flow | about $12.0bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $12.5bn; lower because of a real estate transaction in Q4 |

The revenue forecast for the fourth quarter is only half a percent below expectations. So why does it matter? Because of advertising, which is meant to be Netflix's next source of growth. In October, with little more than a quarter of the year left, the company cut its advertising forecast by a tenth, or $300 million. This year advertising will bring in a little over 5% of revenue and, according to co-CEO Greg Peters, it is growing by more than 80%, but more slowly than targeted.

Profit, however, is still forecast to grow faster than revenue. Operating income is expected to be about 18% higher than a year earlier in the fourth quarter, on revenue 11.5% higher. How that is possible is the core of this analysis.

## Where growth comes from

Growth has gone through a full cycle in recent years. In 2022 and 2023 it nearly stalled, at about 7% a year. Then three boosts arrived at once: paid password sharing from May 2023, price increases and advertising, which grew more than 2.5 times in 2025. In 2024 and 2025 growth was about 16%. This year it is forecast at 13%, and analysts expect 11.2% next year. The boosts are fading and growth is returning towards 10%. The key question is therefore not this year but next.

This year growth comes from three sources: new memberships, higher prices and advertising. Advertising is still small, so the first two show the most.

The biggest market is the US and Canada. At the end of March 2026 Netflix raised the price of all its US plans:

| US plan | Monthly price before | From the end of March 2026 | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard with ads | $7.99 | $8.99 | +12.5% |
| Standard | $17.99 | $19.99 | +11% |
| Premium | $24.99 | $26.99 | +8% |

![US and Canada: growth is slightly higher again after the price increase](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/rast-zda-kanada.svg)

Revenue in the US and Canada grew 11% in the third quarter, the first with the full effect of the price increase. Since prices rose by 8 to 12.5% and advertising adds to growth too, little is left for new memberships. This cannot be checked directly, because Netflix no longer publishes membership numbers.

![Most growth comes from outside the US and Canada](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/regije-delezi.svg)

Outside the US and Canada, where Netflix generates 56% of its revenue and 59% of its growth, growth is easing: excluding currency effects, it slowed in the third quarter in all three regions.

New memberships are linked to viewing, and this is where the tension the company acknowledged lies. Viewing hours grew 2% in the first half of 2026 and 1.5% last year. If memberships grew 5%, each member would be watching less; if members watch as much as before, membership is barely growing. Which explanation holds is the core of the dispute between bears and bulls.

Growth is therefore increasingly carried by prices and advertising, less by new memberships, yet profit is growing faster than revenue. Why?

## Why profit grows faster than revenue

The whole business rests on one property: **a series costs the same whether a hundred million households watch it or three hundred million.** The cost of content is fixed in advance, while revenue grows with every new membership and every price increase. In the reports this cost is called **content amortization**, because Netflix spreads the payment for a film or series over the years in which the content is watched.

![Content cost takes a falling share of revenue, operating margin is rising](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/vsebine-in-marza.svg)

| Item | 2021 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $29.7bn | $45.2bn | +52% |
| Content cost | $12.2bn | $16.4bn | +34% |
| Operating income | $6.2bn | $13.3bn | **+115%** |
| Content cost as a share of revenue | 41.2% | 36.3% | −4.8 points |
| Operating margin | 20.9% | 29.5% | +8.6 points |

Over these four years content cost grew more slowly than revenue, so of every additional dollar of revenue, 46 cents remained as operating income. This is how profit can grow faster than viewing: as long as people stay subscribed and accept higher prices, they do not have to watch more. It also works in reverse: in 2022 content cost grew 14.7% and revenue only 6.5%, and the margin fell.

The mechanism therefore has one condition: revenue has to grow faster than content cost. In 2024 and 2025 the gap was about eight percentage points. This year the company expects revenue growth of 13% and content cost growth of about 10%, so **the gap narrows to about three points.**

![The gap that margin comes from is narrowing this year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/skarje-rasti.svg)

This already shows in the margin. On a comparable basis, excluding last year's one-off charge in Brazil, it is widening ever more slowly:

| Comparable basis | Last year | This year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating margin, first nine months | 33.2% | 33.5% | +0.3 points |
| Operating margin, full year (2026 forecast) | 30.9% | 31.5% | **+0.6 points** |
| Operating income growth, full year | | about 15% | the company cites more than 20% |

The company's forecast of more than 20% growth is higher because last year's base is reduced by a one-off charge (all one-off effects are collected in the next section); the comparable column is an Engineering Investor calculation. The mechanism still works, then, but with an ever smaller cushion: if revenue growth slips below content cost growth, the margin stops widening.

## How much cash remains

Profit in the accounts is not yet cash. **Free cash flow**, cash from operations after investment in equipment, shows something the margin does not.

| First nine months | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content cost in the accounts | $11.66bn | $12.76bn | +9.5% |
| Investment in new content (cash) | $12.04bn | $14.93bn | **+24%** |
| Free cash flow excluding the termination fee | $7.6bn | $7.2bn | lower |

Netflix invested 24% more in new content than last year and considerably more than it expensed. The difference will reach the expense line in the coming years, but it already shows in cash: despite 15% growth in operating income, free cash flow is lower than last year.

![This year more goes into buybacks than there is free cash](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/denar-in-odkupi.svg)

Netflix spends almost all of its free cash flow on **share buybacks**: it buys its own shares on the market and retires them. This year it has spent more on them than it generated in free cash and covered the difference from its cash. In the third quarter it bought at an average price of about $75 (Engineering Investor calculation), and authorised buybacks of 7.7% of market value remain.

Buybacks are not just a treadmill covering stock-based compensation. That compensation amounted to $405 million over nine months, about 4% of free cash flow, while the diluted share count fell 3.3% in one year. Each shareholder therefore owns a larger part of the profit, and a lower share price makes buybacks even more effective, because the same money retires more shares.

Three one-off effects distort this year's figures:

| One-off effect | Amount | In this analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Tax dispute in Brazil (Q3 2025) | charge of $619m | excluded from last year's operating income |
| Warner Bros. termination fee (Q1 2026) | $2.8bn, $2.26bn after tax ($0.53 per share) | deducted from profit and cash |
| Real estate transaction (Q4 2026) | outlay of about $0.5bn | kept as an actual outlay |

Netflix received the termination fee in February 2026, when it walked away from buying part of Warner Bros. Discovery. Excluding the fee, this year's free cash flow is about **$9.7 billion**, and about $10.2 billion without the real estate transaction.

Three more numbers describe the business's returns and risks:

- **Return on invested capital (ROIC)** is about **38%**: every dollar of equity and borrowed capital in the business generates about 38 cents of after-tax profit. The better-known ROE is less useful here, because buybacks and the termination fee inflate it artificially.
- **Debt is small.** Net debt of $7.1 billion is a little under half of one year's operating income.
- **Content is committed in advance.** Contractual content obligations amount to **$25.2 billion**, half of annual revenue, and have to be paid even if viewers leave.

The business is profitable and carries little debt, but it has to put ever more cash into content. What it gets for that depends on the people who choose the content, and those are exactly the people Netflix has just changed.

## Management changes direction

On 14 October management announced it would lay off about 6% of staff, the first time since 2022, according to the company to free up money to invest where it matters most. A day later Netflix announced that Bela Bajaria, who had been at the company for nearly ten years, most recently leading content, is leaving. She is replaced by **Peter Rice**.

Netflix gives no reason for the change; who it chose says more. Rice most recently ran Disney's television and general entertainment business, where series such as *The Bear*, *Shōgun* and *Abbott Elementary* were made under his leadership. Before that, as president of Fox Searchlight, he released films such as *Slumdog Millionaire*, *Juno* and *Little Miss Sunshine*. On his appointment he said Netflix is "willing to take chances on distinctive voices and fresh ideas".

Actions say more than statements: in September Wells Fargo named the lack of big original series as the main problem, and Netflix has chosen someone whose career includes exactly such series and films. Bulls read this as a response to the shortage of hits, bears as an admission that content has not worked. The first series and films under the new leadership will reach screens only in a year or two.

What remains open is the question from the introduction: whether viewing will hold up long enough for the mechanism to keep working. This is where opinions part.

## The bear case: viewers are going elsewhere

The bear case starts with viewers' time. Netflix competes for it with everyone at once, and among its rivals it lists Alphabet (owner of YouTube), Amazon and TikTok. For Alphabet and Amazon, video is only part of a broader business. Nielsen measures what share of their TV time Americans spend on each service:

![Netflix is second in the US, but lost a point in a year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/gledanost-zda.svg)

| Share of TV time in the US (Nielsen) | July 2025 | July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | 13.4% | 14.2% (record) | +0.8 points |
| Netflix | 8.8% (record) | **7.8%** | **−1.0 points** |

The comparison is somewhat unfair to Netflix: according to Nielsen, July 2025 was its record month (Squid Game), while July 2026 was marked by the end of the football World Cup. A drop of a full point therefore probably overstates the trend, but YouTube's record despite the World Cup remains a strong argument.

Netflix's own data points the same way. In 2024 the company cited about two hours of viewing per membership per day; in the first half of 2026 it was about 1.6 (Engineering Investor calculation). On this data, two banks downgraded the stock in September: Wells Fargo to a rating equivalent to "sell", with a price target of $57, and HSBC to "hold", with a price target of $76.

Since growth in the US is carried mainly by price, the bears' question becomes: how much further can prices rise before people start cancelling? The logic of the case runs through the same mechanism as above, only in reverse:

1. Less viewing means a membership is worth less to a household.
2. A membership worth less is harder to raise in price and easier to cancel.
3. Revenue growth slows, while content cost is fixed in advance.
4. A fix costs money: more original content, more licensed content and sport, or an acquisition.

The outcome would be a repeat of 2022, when cost grew faster than revenue and the margin fell.

The bears are also helped by the fact that their case will become harder to test: Netflix no longer publishes membership numbers, and from 2027 the viewing report will come out only once a year.

A deeper reason for concern lies in costs: most content on YouTube is made by users, so only Netflix carries the risk that a title will fail.

## The bull case: the frightening numbers have another explanation

The bulls do not deny that viewing is growing slowly, but argue that the business is not suffering from it.

**If YouTube were taking members, it would show in cancellations.** People do not cancel because they watch something else, but when the subscription is no longer worth it to them. According to the company, the share of members who cancel improved in the third quarter globally and in the US, despite the March price increase. Revenue growth in the US and Canada even accelerated after it, from 10% to 11%. The bear case has to explain why members are not leaving.

**YouTube and Netflix complement each other more than they exclude each other.** YouTube is a daily habit: podcasts, commentary, tutorials, clips at lunch or on the go. Netflix is the place for stories, series and films watched by the household. Someone who spends more time on YouTube can keep Netflix for the one series they follow. The deciding variable is therefore the quality of Netflix's content, not YouTube's share.

**Hours per membership fell because there are more memberships.** This answers the simple example above. When Netflix began charging for password sharing in May 2023, people on other accounts opened their own: there were 260 million memberships at the end of 2023 and 302 million at the end of 2024. Their viewing had already been counted in hours, so it was spread across more memberships.

![Many more memberships, only slightly more viewing hours](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/narocnine-in-ure.svg)

**Nielsen measures one screen in one country,** while Netflix generates more than half of its revenue elsewhere. In India, with a strong slate of local content, viewing hours grew 24% in the third quarter and revenue excluding currency effects 18%.

**The slowdown is mainly the comparison base.** This year Netflix is being compared with years of one-off boosts, described in the section on growth; in the US and Canada, growth jumped to 18% after the price increases at the start of 2025.

**Rivals are indebted.** According to the Financial Times, the combined Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery will have about $80 billion of debt, which will make it harder to increase content spending. That rivals already sell their titles to Netflix is shown by Disney, which has started licensing its films and series to it.

## What analysts think

Most analysts remain on the bull side, but with lower targets after the results:

| Analysts | Value | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Number of analysts (S&P Global) | **52**, consensus "buy" | Most remain on the bull side |
| Average price target | $91.39 | 33% above the price of $68.69 |
| Range of targets | from **$57** to **$126** | Disagreement by more than a factor of 2 |

After the results, BofA, UBS and Deutsche Bank, among others, cut their targets. Morningstar, which estimates fair value, kept it at $80 and still expects double-digit annual profit growth.

## What the price already includes

![Netflix: share price over the last 12 months](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/cena-12-mesecev.svg)

All ratios in this analysis are based on the last close before the results, **$68.69** (20 Oct 2026). The analysis is written for long-term investors, so it does not follow the swings in the days after the results.

Once the termination fee is deducted, the stock is more expensive than it first appears:

| At a price of $68.69 | As reported | Excluding the fee |
|---|---|---|
| P/E, last 12 months | 19.9 ($3.46) | **23.4** ($2.93) |
| P/E 2026 | 19.2 ($3.57) | **22.6** ($3.04) |
| P/E 2027 (analysts) | 18.1 ($3.80) | 18.1 ($3.80) |
| Price/free cash flow 2026 | 24 ($12.0bn) | **28 to 30** |

The fee thus makes past profit look higher and future growth lower. The 2027 earnings estimate is about 6% above this year's earnings as reported by the company, and **about 25%** above this year's earnings excluding the fee.

A more useful question than whether the stock is "cheap" is the one from the introduction, translated into numbers: **what earnings growth does Netflix need for a purchase at $68.69 to deliver a decent return?**

![What has to hold for a purchase at this price to work out](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/scenariji-donosa.svg)

The starting point is this year's earnings per share excluding the termination fee, about $3.04. The table shows the annual return over five years for different earnings growth rates and the price/earnings ratio the market would pay in five years:

- **8% growth and a ratio of 15** → −0.5% a year.
- **12% growth and a ratio of 20** → 9.3% a year.
- **16% growth and a ratio of 25** → 18.4% a year.

What is realistic? Even 10% revenue growth at an unchanged margin, together with buybacks, would mean earnings per share growth of about 13 to 14%. For more, revenue has to keep outpacing content cost, which is again the question from the introduction.

The market has already cut the multiple sharply over one year. According to Deutsche Bank, in June 2025 the stock traded at about 40 times expected earnings; today it is about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings. The price therefore already assumes slower growth.

This creates an asymmetry. On the downside, the worst case in the table gives a return of about zero; an outcome with a shrinking margin is not in the table and would mean a loss. On the upside, earnings per share excluding the fee are growing about 20% this year, and analysts expect even faster growth next year; if growth like that lasted several years, the return would move towards the favourable case. Which outcome is closer depends mainly on whether Netflix has hits again.

To compare with other investments: free cash flow excluding the termination fee and after stock-based compensation amounts to about 3.2% of market value. That is what an owner would receive each year if the company paid out all its cash and stopped growing.

## What to watch to know who is right

All five points are different views of the same question: can profit keep growing faster than viewing?

1. **The gap between revenue growth and content cost growth.** About three points this year. If it closes, the margin stops widening.
2. **Member cancellations.** The company reports them descriptively, against last year and against its forecast. If they started to worsen, that would be the first evidence for the bear case.
3. **Revenue growth in the US and Canada.** +11% in the third quarter, mostly because of the price increase. Once its effect fades in spring 2027, it will show how much new memberships contribute.
4. **Content investment relative to the expense in the accounts.** The ratio was 1.17 over nine months. A higher ratio without faster growth means that keeping viewers is getting more expensive.
5. **Viewing hours and the first content under the new chief.** The next viewing report comes in early 2027, the first content under Peter Rice in a year or two.

The next milestone is the fourth-quarter results in January 2027, together with the first forecast for 2027.

## A conclusion everyone has to draw for themselves

The third quarter confirmed both stories at once. The business works: revenue is growing at double digits in every region, the margin is still widening and the share count is falling. At the same time, growth is increasingly carried by prices, free cash flow excluding the fee is lower than last year, and the company itself has admitted that **viewing is not growing as fast as the money**.

Anyone who thinks slow viewing is noise today pays about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings for a business with a 38% return on capital. Anyone who thinks it is an early sign sees $25 billion of content committed in advance, prices that cannot rise forever and a margin that is widening by only a little over half a point a year. Who is right will be shown by the gap between revenue growth and content cost growth, and by the first hits under the new leadership.
