Netflix reported its third-quarter results on 20 October, after the US market closed. Revenue was in line with its forecast and profit above it, but for the final quarter of the year the company forecast less revenue than analysts had expected and cut its forecast for advertising revenue. In after-hours trading the stock lost about 6%. A week earlier, Netflix had announced it would lay off about 6% of its staff and replaced its content chief. This analysis looks at what the results confirm, what they change and what the price already includes.

## What the third quarter brought

| Third quarter | A year earlier | Company forecast (16 Jul) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $11.51bn | $12.86bn | $12.87bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.2% | +11.7% | +11.8% |
| Operating margin | 28.2% | 33.2% | **34.6%** |
| Earnings per share | $0.59 | $0.82 | **$0.87** |
| Free cash flow | $2.66bn | no forecast | $2.88bn |

Two terms come up repeatedly below. **Operating income** is profit from the business itself: revenue minus all operating costs, before interest and taxes. **Operating margin** shows what share of revenue remains as operating income.

Revenue landed exactly on the forecast, margin and profit above it. The company attributes the margin difference mainly to the timing of expenses, meaning some costs come later than planned. Operating income grew 37%, but last year's third quarter was reduced by a one-off charge of $619 million for a tax dispute in Brazil. Without it, growth is **15%**, and the margin is one point higher (33.6% last year, 34.6% this year).

The forecast for the final quarter and for the full year:

| Fourth quarter | A year earlier | Company forecast | Analyst expectations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $12.05bn | $13.44bn | $13.51bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.6% | +11.5% | not available |
| Operating margin | 24.5% | 26.0% | not available |
| Earnings per share | $0.56 | $0.67 | not available |

| Year 2026 | Forecast of 16 Jul | Forecast of 20 Oct |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $51.0bn to $51.4bn | $51.1bn |
| Operating margin | 31.5% | 31.5% |
| Advertising revenue | about $3bn | about $2.7bn |
| Free cash flow | about $12.5bn | about $12.0bn |

The revenue forecast for the final quarter is half a percent below expectations. The margin for that quarter includes 1.2 points of layoff costs; without them it would be 27.2%. According to the company, subscriptions are doing better than expected this year and advertising worse, because it is rolling out some advertising capabilities more slowly. Free cash flow will be lower because of a real estate transaction that, according to the company, will close in the fourth quarter.

Two organisational changes came alongside the results. On 14 October, management told employees it was laying off about 6% of staff; according to Screen Daily, about 900 people (other sources say about 1,000), the first time since 2022. A day later, Netflix announced that **Peter Rice**, formerly president of Disney's television business and before that head of Fox and of the film studio Fox Searchlight, would become content chief on 26 October. He replaces Bela Bajaria, who led content for almost ten years. The stock rose 2.9% that day.

The last close is **44.7% below the peak** and 26.7% below the price at the start of the year. All prices are adjusted for the stock split: in November 2025, Netflix split each share into ten.

The biggest drops of the past year came the day after quarterly results:

| Day | Change | What was announced the evening before |
|---|---|---|
| 22 Oct 2025 | −10.1% | Q3 2025 results: profit lower because of the Brazilian tax charge |
| 17 Apr 2026 | −9.7% | Q1 2026 results above forecast, but with a forecast of slower growth; co-founder Reed Hastings leaves the board |

The pattern has now repeated for the third time this year: the past quarter matches or beats the company's forecast, and the stock falls on what the company says about the next one. The only big jump up, +13.8% on 27 February 2026, came on the day the acquisition agreement was terminated; that event has its own section below.

## How Netflix makes money: one cost spread over ever more memberships

Netflix sells a monthly subscription to series, films, live events and games, increasingly with advertising. At the end of 2025 it had more than 325 million paid memberships and, according to its latest shareholder letter, reaches almost a billion viewers.

The whole business rests on one property: **a series costs the same whether a hundred million households watch it or three hundred million.** The cost of content is fixed in advance, while revenue grows with every new membership and every price increase. In the reports this cost is called **content amortization**: Netflix does not expense the payment for a film or series immediately, but spreads it over the years in which the content is watched.

![Content cost takes a falling share of revenue, operating margin is rising](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/vsebine-in-marza.svg)

| Item | 2021 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $29.7bn | $45.2bn | +52% |
| Content cost | $12.2bn | $16.4bn | +34% |
| Operating income | $6.2bn | $13.3bn | **+115%** |
| Content cost as a share of revenue | 41.2% | 36.3% | −4.8 points |
| Operating margin | 20.9% | 29.5% | +8.6 points |

Content cost has grown in dollars, but more slowly than revenue, so it takes an ever smaller share of revenue. What remains goes to profit: of every additional dollar of revenue, 46 cents reached operating income.

2022 shows that the mechanism works in both directions. Content cost grew 14.7% that year and revenue only 6.5%, and the margin fell to 17.8%. The content had been ordered and paid for; revenue did not follow.

According to the company, revenue growth has three sources:

1. **New memberships.** By the company's estimate, fewer than 45% of the broadband households it counts as its market have a membership. It has not published membership numbers since 2025.
2. **Higher prices.** At the end of March 2026 it raised the price of all plans in the US. According to the company, members reacted to the increase the same way as to previous ones.
3. **Advertising.** In 2025 it brought in more than $1.5 billion; this year it will be about $2.7 billion, a little over 5% of revenue. In 2027 the ad-supported plan will become available in 15 new countries.

![Most growth comes from outside the US and Canada](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/regije-delezi.svg)

| Region | Q3 2026 revenue | Share | Growth | Growth excluding currency effects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US and Canada | $5.63bn | 44% | +11% | +11% |
| Europe, Middle East and Africa | $4.11bn | 32% | +11% | +9% |
| Latin America | $1.60bn | 12% | +17% | +12% |
| Asia-Pacific | $1.54bn | 12% | +12% | +15% |

The US and Canada bring in 44% of revenue and 41% of its growth. Growth in the region accelerated from 10% in the second quarter to 11%; the third quarter was the first with the full effect of the March price increase. Excluding currency effects, growth slowed in all three regions outside the US and Canada, and Asia-Pacific is still growing fastest.

## The gap that margin comes from is narrowing

![The gap that margin comes from is narrowing this year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/skarje-rasti.svg)

Margin widens only as long as revenue grows faster than content cost. In 2024 and 2025 the gap was about eight percentage points. This year the company expects revenue growth of 13% and content cost growth of about 10%: **the gap narrows to about three points.**

| First nine months | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $33.13bn | $37.68bn | +13.7% |
| Content cost | $11.66bn | $12.76bn | +9.5% |
| Operating income | $10.37bn | $12.61bn | +21.6% |
| Operating income excluding the Brazilian charge | $10.99bn | $12.61bn | +14.7% |
| Operating margin excluding the Brazilian charge | 33.2% | 33.5% | +0.3 points |
| Investment in new content | $12.04bn | $14.93bn | **+24%** |

On a comparable basis, the margin widened by a third of a point over nine months. The last row shows that the gap is larger in cash than in the accounts: Netflix invested considerably more in new content than it expensed, and the difference will reach the expense line in the coming years. In the third quarter, content cost in the accounts grew only 5.8%, investment in new content 10.9%.

Revenue growth is still falling from quarter to quarter, but ever more slowly:

| Quarter | Revenue | Growth year on year |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 2025 | $12.05bn | +17.6% |
| Q1 2026 | $12.25bn | +16.2% |
| Q2 2026 | $12.56bn | +13.4% |
| Q3 2026 | $12.87bn | +11.8% |
| Q4 2026 (company forecast) | $13.44bn | **+11.5%** |

Between the second and third quarters growth fell by 1.6 points; between the third and fourth it is expected to fall by only 0.3 points. Whether it stops there will become clear only with the 2027 forecast, which the company will publish in January.

For the full year, the company forecasts operating income growth of more than 20%. Last year's base for this comparison was reduced by the Brazilian charge, which according to the company relates mostly to the years 2022 to 2024:

| Year 2026 | As the company forecasts | Excluding the Brazilian charge from last year's base |
|---|---|---|
| Operating margin 2025 | 29.5% | 30.9% |
| Operating margin 2026 | 31.5% | 31.5% |
| Margin expansion | 2.0 points | **0.6 points** |
| Operating income growth | more than 20% | **about 15%** |

The right-hand column is an Engineering Investor calculation. This year's forecast already includes 0.3 points of layoff costs. The mechanism still works, then, but with a smaller cushion than in the past two years: if revenue growth slips below content cost growth, the margin stops widening. The key question is whether the slowdown is the start of permanently weaker growth or merely a comparison with an exceptionally strong year. This is exactly where the bear and bull cases below part ways.

## A termination fee that distorts profit in both directions

On 5 December 2025, Netflix announced that it would buy the film and streaming business of Warner Bros. Discovery for $82.7 billion (including debt). Paramount Skydance offered more for the whole company. Netflix did not raise its offer; co-CEO Ted Sarandos later said they walked away when the price exceeded the deal's value for shareholders. The agreement was terminated on 27 February 2026 and Netflix received a **termination fee of $2.8 billion**.

![In one quarter, net income jumped above operating income](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/dobicek-in-odstopnina.svg)

The termination fee is not revenue from operations, so it does not change operating income. First-quarter net income, however, jumped to $5.28 billion with it. Netflix had forecast earnings per share of $0.76 for that quarter and reported $1.23; the difference, **$0.47**, is almost entirely explained by the fee.

| Price/earnings ratio at a price of $68.69 | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| On earnings for the last 12 months ($3.46) | 19.9 | Includes the termination fee |
| The same, excluding the fee ($2.99) | **23.0** | Engineering Investor estimate |
| On 2026 earnings ($3.57) | 19.2 | Nine months and the Q4 forecast |
| The same, excluding the fee ($3.10) | **22.2** | Starting point for the calculation below |
| On estimated 2027 earnings ($3.80) | **18.1** | Analyst estimate before the results |

The fee distorts two things at once. **Past profit looks higher:** ratios of around 19 and 20 are lower than they would be without the one-off receipt. **Future growth looks lower,** because this year's base contains the fee and next year's will not:

| Earnings per share | 2026 | 2027 estimate | Growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| As reported by the company | $3.57 | $3.80 | about +6% |
| Excluding the fee | $3.10 | $3.80 | **about +23%** |

The 2027 estimate dates from before the results. After the weaker fourth-quarter forecast, analysts will probably revise it; by how much is not known today.

## The cash that actually remains

![This year more goes into buybacks than there is free cash](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/denar-in-odkupi.svg)

**Free cash flow** is cash from operations minus investment in equipment. Almost all of it goes into **share buybacks**: the company buys its own shares on the market and retires them, so each remaining share represents a larger part of the company.

| Item | 2024 | 2025 | First nine months of 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free cash flow | $6.92bn | $9.46bn | $9.50bn |
| Share buybacks | $6.26bn | $9.13bn | **$10.78bn** |

This year Netflix has spent more on buybacks than it generated in free cash. It covered the difference from its cash, which has fallen from $9.0 billion to $8.2 billion since the start of the year; a $1 billion bond issued in the third quarter will be used to repay a bond maturing in the fourth quarter.

In the third quarter it bought back 63.9 million shares for $4.8 billion, the most in a single quarter so far. The average price was about $75 (Engineering Investor calculation). Anyone buying at the last close pays 8.5% less than the company itself paid in the third quarter. Authorised buybacks of $22.3 billion remain, 7.7% of market value, and the share count has fallen 3.3% in one year.

| Free cash flow | Amount | Market value relative to it |
|---|---|---|
| Year 2025 | $9.46bn | 30 times |
| Forecast for 2026 | about $12.0bn | **24 times** |

This year's forecast includes the termination fee and a one-off real estate transaction, which work in opposite directions. Stock-based compensation, which free cash flow does not deduct, amounts to about 4% of free cash flow.

Three numbers for confirmation:

- **Return on invested capital (ROIC)**, the after-tax profit on every dollar of equity and borrowed capital in the business, is about **38%**. The better-known return on equity (ROE) is less useful here, because buybacks and the termination fee inflate it artificially.
- **Debt is small.** Net debt is $7.1 billion, a little under half of one year's operating income. It has grown since June, because buybacks exceeded free cash.
- **Content is committed in advance.** Netflix has **$25.2 billion** of contractual content obligations, half of annual revenue. This is the other side of a fixed cost: content has to be paid for regardless of how many members remain.

## The bear case: viewers are going elsewhere

Bears start with competition. Netflix competes for viewers' free time with everyone at once; among its rivals it lists Alphabet (owner of YouTube), Amazon, Apple, Comcast, Disney, Meta, Roblox and TikTok. The strongest evidence for this case is not in Netflix's reports but in viewing measurements.

![Netflix is second in the US, but lost a point in a year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/gledanost-zda.svg)

Every month Nielsen measures what share of their TV time Americans spend on each service:

| Share of TV time in the US | July 2025 | July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | 13.4% | 14.2% (record) | +0.8 points |
| Netflix | 8.8% | **7.8%** | **−1.0 points** |

These are the latest published figures. Netflix's own data points the same way: total viewing hours grew only 2% in the first half of 2026, while memberships grew faster. In 2024 the company cited about two hours of viewing per paid membership per day; in the first half of 2026 it was about 1.6 (Engineering Investor calculation).

What is new in the third quarter is that the company put the concern in writing itself. In its shareholder letter it says it is aware of investors' concern about growth in viewing hours and wants to accelerate it. CFO Spence Neumann added on the call with analysts that they are not satisfied with growth in viewing hours.

Wells Fargo analyst Steven Cahall built his September downgrade on exactly this, to a rating equivalent to "sell". In his view, Netflix lacks big original series, and new hits are essential for the stock to recover; he cut his price target to $57.

The logic of the case runs through the same mechanism as above, only in reverse:

1. Less viewing means a membership is worth less to a household.
2. A membership worth less is harder to raise in price and easier to cancel.
3. Revenue growth slows, while content cost is fixed in advance.
4. A fix costs money: more original content, more licensed content and sport, or an acquisition.

The outcome would be a repeat of 2022: cost grows faster than revenue and the margin falls.

Bears find four more supports in the latest results. The fourth-quarter forecast is below expectations, advertising will bring in a tenth less than forecast this year, with the fourth-quarter forecast revenue growth will slow for the fourth quarter in a row, and the company is publishing less data: it has not published membership numbers since 2025, and from 2027 the viewing report will come out once a year instead of twice. These are precisely the two measures that could test the bear case. The layoffs and the change of content chief can also be read as an admission that content has not worked as it should.

There is a deeper reason for concern. Most content on YouTube is created by users themselves, so YouTube does not pay for it in advance and does not carry the risk that a title will fail. Netflix carries that risk in full.

## The bull case: the frightening numbers have another explanation

Management no longer denies the viewing problem, but argues that the business is not suffering from it. It has some evidence for this in its reports.

**Members are not leaving.** According to the company, the share of members who cancel improved in the third quarter both globally and in the US, where it was better than forecast. Members accepted the price increase the same way as previous ones, and revenue growth in the US and Canada accelerated from 10% to 11% in the first full quarter after it. People watching Netflix less because it was worth less to them would cancel more often at a higher price.

**Hours per membership fell because there are more memberships, not because people watch less.** In May 2023 Netflix began charging for password sharing outside the household. People who had watched on someone else's account opened their own: there were 260 million memberships at the end of 2023 and 302 million at the end of 2024. Their viewing had already been counted in total hours, so the same number of hours was spread across more memberships.

![Many more memberships, only slightly more viewing hours](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/narocnine-in-ure.svg)

**Nielsen measures one screen in one country.** Netflix generates 56% of its revenue and 59% of its growth outside the US and Canada. An example from the third quarter is India: with a strong slate of local content, viewing hours there grew 24%, hours per membership by a few percent, revenue excluding currency effects 18%, and India had its most new memberships ever. Where content works, viewing moves.

**Not all hours are worth the same.** Live events account for only about 1% of viewing hours, yet six of the ten days with the most new members in the past five years were linked to them. People watch a live event for a short time but subscribe because of it, and it also attracts advertisers. The fourth quarter will have the most live events yet, including three NFL games.

**The slowdown is mainly the comparison base.** Growth over the past two years was driven by two boosts that do not repeat: paid password sharing in 2024 and the price increases at the start of 2025, after which growth in the US and Canada jumped to 18%. This year Netflix is being compared with exactly those quarters.

![US and Canada: growth is slightly higher again after the price increase](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/rast-zda-kanada.svg)

**The layoffs and the new content chief are a response, not a symptom.** According to Screen Daily, the layoffs mainly affect product development, technology, legal and human resources, and according to the company they free up money to invest where it matters most. Peter Rice ran the film studio Fox Searchlight, and later Fox and Disney's television business. To bulls, this is a sign that management takes the shortage of hits seriously and is solving it within the company, not through an acquisition.

**Rivals are indebted.** Paramount, which is acquiring Warner Bros. Discovery, issued $52 billion of debt for the deal; according to the Financial Times, the combined company will have about $80 billion of debt. A company with that much debt will find it harder to increase content spending and has a strong reason to sell its titles everywhere, including to its biggest rival. That is already happening: Disney has started licensing its films and series to Netflix, among them all five Ice Age films.

What analysts show:

| Measure | Value | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Analysts with a price target | **32**, consensus "buy" | Most remain on the bull side |
| Average price target | $90.69 | +32% from the close of 20 Oct |
| Range of targets | from **$57** to **$125** | Disagreement by a factor of 2.2 |

The targets date from before the results. Five banks had already lowered their price targets in October, before the results.

## What the price already includes

![What has to hold for a purchase at this price to work out](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/scenariji-donosa.svg)

Instead of judging whether the stock is "cheap", a more useful question is: **what does Netflix have to achieve for a purchase at $68.69 to deliver a decent return?**

The starting point is this year's earnings per share excluding the termination fee, about **$3.10**, which at this price means a price/earnings ratio of **22.2**. The table shows the annual return over the next five years for different combinations of earnings growth and the ratio at which the market would value the stock in five years:

- **8% growth and a ratio of 15 in five years** → about 0% a year. Nothing.
- **12% growth and a ratio of 20** → 9.7% a year.
- **16% growth and a ratio of 25** → 18.8% a year.

What is realistic for growth? Over the past two years, earnings per share grew faster than any row in the table (+65% and +28%), and before the results analysts expected about 23% for 2027 on a comparable basis. Even 10% revenue growth at an unchanged margin, together with buybacks (last year they reduced the share count by 3.3%), would mean earnings per share growth of about 13 to 14%.

Over one year the market has sharply cut Netflix's multiple: according to Deutsche Bank, at its peak in June 2025 the stock traded at about 40 times expected earnings, and today at about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings. A multiple cut that far means the price already assumes slower growth. It does not, however, protect against an outcome in which the margin starts to shrink: if earnings grow less than 8% a year and the market pays 15 times for them, the return is zero.

After the results, the price fell about 6% in after-hours trading. If it settles there, all the ratios in this analysis are lower by that much.

## What to watch to know who is right

1. **The gap between revenue growth and content cost growth.** About three points this year. If it closes, the margin stops widening, regardless of everything else.
2. **Revenue growth in the US and Canada.** +11% in the third quarter. The fourth quarter is compared with last year's 18%; if growth stays above 10%, the comparison-base explanation gains weight.
3. **Margin on a comparable basis.** The fourth-quarter forecast (26.0%) includes layoff costs; without them it is 27.2%, against 24.5% last year.
4. **Content investment relative to the expense in the accounts.** Over nine months the ratio was 1.17; for the full year the company forecasts about 1.1. A higher ratio without faster growth means that keeping viewers is getting more expensive.
5. **Viewing hours.** Netflix's next global viewing report comes in early 2027, and Nielsen is changing its measurement method this autumn, so the new figures will not be directly comparable with the old ones.
6. **The first content under new leadership.** Series and films commissioned by Peter Rice will reach screens only in a year or two; the first signs will be in the forecasts for 2027.

The next milestone is the fourth-quarter results in January 2027 (date not yet announced), together with the first forecast for 2027:

| Fourth quarter | Last year | Company forecast for this year |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $12.05bn | $13.44bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.6% | +11.5% |
| Operating margin | 24.5% | 26.0% |
| Operating margin excluding layoff costs | 24.5% | 27.2% |
| Earnings per share | $0.56 | $0.67 |

## A conclusion everyone has to draw for themselves

The third quarter did not change the picture; it confirmed both stories at once. The business works: revenue is growing at double digits in every region, the margin is still widening on a comparable basis, members cancel less often, and buybacks are at a record. And the question that pushed the stock 45% below its peak has now been put in writing by the company itself: **viewing is not growing as fast as the money.**

Anyone who thinks this is noise (one market, one screen, more memberships sharing the same hours, India as proof that content still works) today pays about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings for a business with a 38% return on capital, revenue growing 12% and a stock the company itself was buying at about $75 in the third quarter. Anyone who thinks it is an early sign (YouTube growing, fewer hits, advertising lagging, management changing people and publishing less data) is looking at a company with $25 billion of content committed in advance and a margin that is widening by only a little over half a point a year on a comparable basis; in that case even this multiple is not cheap.

No single set of results will give the answer. It will come from the gap between revenue growth and content cost growth in the coming years, the very number through which Netflix doubled its profit over the past four years, and from the first content of the new content chief.
