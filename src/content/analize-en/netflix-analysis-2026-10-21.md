Netflix reported its third-quarter results on 20 October, after the US market closed. Profit beat the forecast, but the forecast for the fourth quarter came in below analysts' expectations. In its shareholder letter the company wrote for the first time that it is aware of investors' concern about growth in viewing hours.

That concern is in the title of this analysis. The money Netflix earns is growing much faster than the time people spend with its content. The analysis therefore follows one question: **how long can profit keep growing faster than viewing, and how much of that growth is already in the price?**

## What the third quarter brought

The quarter itself delivered on the forecast. Revenue landed on the forecast, margin and profit above it:

| Third quarter | A year earlier | Company forecast (16 Jul) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $11.51bn | $12.86bn | $12.87bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.2% | +11.7% | +11.8% |
| Operating margin | 28.2% | 33.2% | **34.6%** |
| Earnings per share | $0.59 | $0.82 | **$0.87** |
| Free cash flow | $2.66bn | no forecast | $2.88bn |

**Operating income** is profit from the business itself, before interest and taxes. **Operating margin** shows what share of revenue remains as operating income. The company attributes the higher-than-forecast margin mainly to the timing of expenses: some costs come later than planned.

Operating income grew 37%, but that figure overstates it. Last year's third quarter was reduced by a one-off charge of $619 million for a tax dispute in Brazil. Without it, growth is **15%**, which is still well ahead of revenue.

What the company said about the future was weaker:

| Company forecast of 20 Oct | Forecast | For comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 revenue | $13.44bn (+11.5%) | analysts: $13.51bn |
| Q4 operating margin | 26.0% (27.2% excluding layoff costs) | last year: 24.5% |
| 2026 revenue | $51.1bn | forecast of 16 Jul: $51.0bn to $51.4bn |
| 2026 advertising revenue | about $2.7bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $3bn |
| 2026 free cash flow | about $12.0bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $12.5bn; lower because of a real estate transaction in Q4 |

The gap to analysts' expectations is small, half a percent, but the forecast means revenue growth keeps slowing. The company attributes the shortfall in advertising to a slower rollout of some advertising capabilities, while it says subscriptions are growing better than expected.

A week before the results, management announced it would lay off about 6% of staff, the first time since 2022, and named a new content chief, **Peter Rice**, formerly president of Disney's television business. According to the company, the layoffs free up money to invest where it matters most.

## The price shows when the market changed its mind

![Netflix: share price over the last 12 months](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/cena-12-mesecev.svg)

The last close before the results, **$68.69** (20 Oct 2026), is 44.7% below the 12-month peak and 26.7% below the price at the start of the year. All ratios in this analysis are based on that close; the analysis is written for long-term investors, so it does not follow the swings in the days after the results.

The pattern of the past year is still instructive. The stock fell on the day after every set of results:

| Day | Change | What was announced the evening before |
|---|---|---|
| 22 Oct 2025 | −10.1% | Q3 2025 results: profit lower because of the Brazilian tax charge |
| 21 Jan 2026 | −2.2% | Q4 2025 results and the first forecast for 2026, with growth of 12 to 14% after 16% in 2025 |
| 17 Apr 2026 | −9.7% | Q1 2026 results above forecast, a forecast of slower growth; co-founder Reed Hastings leaves the board |
| 17 Jul 2026 | −7.3% | Q2 2026 results in line with forecast, a forecast of even slower growth; viewing data to be published less often |

After the third-quarter results the stock fell again in after-hours trading. This year every quarter has matched or beaten the forecast, and every time the fall came on the day after the company said how it would grow from here. For an investor, the question that matters more than any single quarter is therefore how long growth can last. The answer lies in how Netflix's profit is made.

## How Netflix makes money: one cost spread over ever more memberships

Netflix sells a monthly subscription to series, films, live events and games, increasingly with advertising. At the end of 2025 it had more than 325 million paid memberships.

The whole business rests on one property: **a series costs the same whether a hundred million households watch it or three hundred million.** The cost of content is fixed in advance, while revenue grows with every new membership and every price increase. In the reports this cost is called **content amortization**, because Netflix spreads the payment for a film or series over the years in which the content is watched.

![Content cost takes a falling share of revenue, operating margin is rising](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/vsebine-in-marza.svg)

| Item | 2021 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $29.7bn | $45.2bn | +52% |
| Content cost | $12.2bn | $16.4bn | +34% |
| Operating income | $6.2bn | $13.3bn | **+115%** |
| Content cost as a share of revenue | 41.2% | 36.3% | −4.8 points |
| Operating margin | 20.9% | 29.5% | +8.6 points |

Over these four years content cost grew more slowly than revenue, so of every additional dollar of revenue, 46 cents remained as operating income. This is how profit can grow faster than viewing: as long as people stay subscribed and accept higher prices, they do not have to watch more.

The mechanism also works in reverse. In 2022 content cost grew 14.7% and revenue only 6.5%, and the margin fell to 17.8%.

Revenue grows from three sources: new memberships, higher prices and advertising. Advertising is the smallest but fastest-growing source. This year it will bring in about $2.7 billion, a little over 5% of revenue, and according to co-CEO Greg Peters it is growing by more than 80%.

## The gap that margin comes from is narrowing

The mechanism has one condition: revenue has to grow faster than content cost. In 2024 and 2025 the gap was about eight percentage points. This year the company expects revenue growth of 13% and content cost growth of about 10%, so **the gap narrows to about three points.**

![The gap that margin comes from is narrowing this year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/skarje-rasti.svg)

| First nine months | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $33.13bn | $37.68bn | +13.7% |
| Content cost | $11.66bn | $12.76bn | +9.5% |
| Operating income excluding the Brazilian charge | $10.99bn | $12.61bn | +14.7% |
| Operating margin excluding the Brazilian charge | 33.2% | 33.5% | +0.3 points |
| Investment in new content | $12.04bn | $14.93bn | **+24%** |

On a comparable basis, the margin widened by only a third of a point over nine months. The last row explains why the gap is even larger in cash. Netflix invested 24% more in new content than last year and considerably more than it expensed, and the difference will reach the expense line in the coming years.

This shows up in free cash flow. Despite 15% growth in operating income, nine-month free cash flow excluding the termination fee (explained in the next section) was about $7.2 billion, less than in the same period last year ($7.6 billion).

Revenue growth meanwhile is still slowing, but ever more slowly: 11.8% in the third quarter, a forecast 11.5% for the fourth, and analysts expect 11.2% next year. For the full year 2026 the company forecasts operating income growth of more than 20%, but this figure too is inflated by last year's Brazilian charge:

| Year 2026 | As the company forecasts | Excluding the Brazilian charge from last year's base |
|---|---|---|
| Operating margin 2025 | 29.5% | 30.9% |
| Operating margin 2026 | 31.5% | 31.5% |
| Margin expansion | 2.0 points | **0.6 points** |
| Operating income growth | more than 20% | **about 15%** |

The right-hand column is an Engineering Investor calculation. The mechanism still works, then, but with an ever smaller cushion. If revenue growth slips below content cost growth, the margin stops widening and profit can no longer grow faster than the business.

## A termination fee that distorts profit and cash flow

Before looking at what the stock is worth, one more one-off effect has to be set aside. In December 2025 Netflix agreed to buy the film and streaming business of Warner Bros. Discovery, but did not raise its offer when Paramount Skydance offered more for the whole company. When the agreement was terminated in February 2026, Netflix received a **termination fee of $2.8 billion**.

The fee is not revenue from operations, so it does not change operating income, but it raises net income and cash flow. At the first quarter's effective tax rate, it added about **$0.53** to earnings per share. Without it, first-quarter earnings per share would have been about $0.70 on this calculation; the forecast was $0.76.

| Price/earnings ratio at a price of $68.69 | As reported by the company | Excluding the fee |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings for the last 12 months | 19.9 ($3.46) | **23.4** ($2.93) |
| 2026 earnings (nine months and the Q4 forecast) | 19.2 ($3.57) | **22.6** ($3.04) |
| Analyst estimate for 2027 | 18.1 ($3.80) | 18.1 ($3.80) |

The fee thus makes past profit look higher and future growth lower. The 2027 estimate is about 6% above this year's earnings as reported by the company, and **about 25%** above this year's earnings excluding the fee.

The same goes for cash. In April the company raised its free cash flow forecast for this year from $11 billion to $12.5 billion, mainly because of the after-tax fee, and in October cut it to $12.0 billion because of the real estate transaction. Excluding the fee, this year's free cash flow is about **$9.7 billion**. The real estate transaction stays in this figure because it is an actual outlay; without it the figure would be about $10.2 billion. Market value is **28 to 30 times** that amount, not 24 times as the forecast suggests.

## Where the remaining cash goes

![This year more goes into buybacks than there is free cash](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/denar-in-odkupi.svg)

**Free cash flow** is cash from operations minus investment in equipment. Netflix spends almost all of it on **share buybacks**: it buys its own shares on the market and retires them, so each remaining share represents a larger part of the company.

| Item | 2024 | 2025 | First nine months of 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free cash flow | $6.92bn | $9.46bn | $9.50bn |
| Share buybacks | $6.26bn | $9.13bn | **$10.78bn** |

This year Netflix has spent more on buybacks than it generated in free cash and covered the difference from its cash. In the third quarter it bought back 63.9 million shares at an average price of about $75 (Engineering Investor calculation). Anyone buying at $68.69 pays 8.5% less than the company did in the third quarter. Authorised buybacks of $22.3 billion remain, 7.7% of market value. Stock-based compensation, which free cash flow does not deduct, amounted to $405 million over nine months, about 4% of free cash flow.

Three more numbers describe the business's returns and risks:

- **Return on invested capital (ROIC)** is about **38%**: every dollar of equity and borrowed capital in the business generates about 38 cents of after-tax profit. The better-known return on equity (ROE) is less useful here, because buybacks and the termination fee inflate it artificially.
- **Debt is small.** Net debt of $7.1 billion is a little under half of one year's operating income.
- **Content is committed in advance.** Contractual content obligations amount to **$25.2 billion**, half of annual revenue. This is the other side of the mechanism: the cost is fixed in advance even if viewers leave.

The business is profitable and carries little debt. What remains open is the question from the introduction: whether viewing will hold up long enough for the mechanism to keep working. This is where opinions part.

## The bear case: viewers are going elsewhere

The bear case starts with viewers' time. Netflix competes for it with everyone at once, and among its rivals it lists Alphabet (owner of YouTube) and TikTok. Nielsen measures what share of their TV time Americans spend on each service:

![Netflix is second in the US, but lost a point in a year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/gledanost-zda.svg)

| Share of TV time in the US (Nielsen) | July 2025 | July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | 13.4% | 14.2% (record) | +0.8 points |
| Netflix | 8.8% (record) | **7.8%** | **−1.0 points** |

The comparison is somewhat unfair to Netflix. According to Nielsen, July 2025 was its record month, with Squid Game as the most watched title, while July 2026 was marked by the end of the football World Cup. A drop of a full point therefore probably overstates the trend. That YouTube set a record despite the World Cup remains a strong argument.

Netflix's own data points the same way. Viewing hours grew 2% in the first half of 2026 and 1.5% last year, while memberships grew faster. In 2024 the company cited about two hours of viewing per membership per day; in the first half of 2026 it was about 1.6 (Engineering Investor calculation).

On this data, two banks downgraded the stock in September. Wells Fargo cut it to a rating equivalent to "sell", with a price target of $57, and HSBC to "hold", with a price target of $76.

The logic of the case runs through the same mechanism as above, only in reverse:

1. Less viewing means a membership is worth less to a household.
2. A membership worth less is harder to raise in price and easier to cancel.
3. Revenue growth slows, while content cost is fixed in advance.
4. A fix costs money: more original content, more licensed content and sport, or an acquisition.

The outcome would be a repeat of 2022, when cost grew faster than revenue and the margin fell.

The bears are also helped by the fact that their case will become harder to test. Netflix no longer publishes membership numbers, and from 2027 the viewing report will come out once a year instead of twice.

A deeper reason for concern lies in costs. Most content on YouTube is created by users themselves, so YouTube does not carry the risk that a title will fail. Netflix carries it in full.

## The bull case: the frightening numbers have another explanation

The bulls do not deny that viewing is growing slowly. They argue that the business is not suffering from it, and they have some evidence for this in the reports.

**Members are not leaving.** According to the company, the share of members who cancel improved in the third quarter globally and in the US. Revenue growth in the US and Canada even accelerated in the first full quarter after the March price increase, from 10% to 11%. If a membership really were worth less to people, they would cancel more often at a higher price.

**Hours per membership fell because there are more memberships.** When Netflix began charging for password sharing in May 2023, people who had watched on someone else's account opened their own. There were 260 million memberships at the end of 2023 and 302 million at the end of 2024. Their viewing had already been counted in total hours, so the same number of hours was spread across more memberships.

![Many more memberships, only slightly more viewing hours](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/narocnine-in-ure.svg)

**Nielsen measures one screen in one country.** Netflix generates 56% of its revenue and 59% of its growth outside the US and Canada.

![Most growth comes from outside the US and Canada](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/regije-delezi.svg)

In India, with a strong slate of local content, viewing hours grew 24% in the third quarter and revenue excluding currency effects 18%.

**Not all hours are worth the same.** This year live events will take a little over 5% of content spending and bring only about 1% of viewing hours. A live hour is therefore expensive, yet six of the ten days with the most new members in the past five years were linked to live events. An hour that brings in a new member is worth more than an hour that merely fills an existing member's evening.

**The slowdown is mainly the comparison base.** Growth over the past two years was driven by paid password sharing in 2024 and the price increases at the start of 2025, after which growth in the US and Canada jumped to 18%. This year Netflix is being compared with exactly those quarters.

![US and Canada: growth is slightly higher again after the price increase](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/rast-zda-kanada.svg)

**Rivals are indebted.** Paramount issued $52 billion of debt to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery, and according to the Financial Times the combined company will have about $80 billion of debt. Such a rival will find it harder to increase content spending and has a reason to sell its titles to others. That this is more than theory is shown by Disney, which has already started licensing its films and series to Netflix.

## What the price already includes

Instead of judging whether the stock is "cheap", a more useful question is the one from the introduction, translated into numbers: **what earnings growth does Netflix need for a purchase at $68.69 to deliver a decent return?**

![What has to hold for a purchase at this price to work out](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/scenariji-donosa.svg)

The starting point is this year's earnings per share excluding the termination fee, about $3.04. The table shows the annual return over the next five years for different earnings growth rates and different price/earnings ratios the market would pay in five years. Three examples:

- **8% growth and a ratio of 15** → −0.5% a year.
- **12% growth and a ratio of 20** → 9.3% a year.
- **16% growth and a ratio of 25** → 18.4% a year.

What is realistic? Even 10% revenue growth at an unchanged margin, together with buybacks, would mean earnings per share growth of about 13 to 14%. For more than that, the margin has to keep widening, which means revenue has to keep outpacing content cost. We are back at the question from the introduction.

The market has already cut the multiple sharply over one year. According to Deutsche Bank, in June 2025 the stock traded at about 40 times expected earnings; today it is about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings. The price therefore already assumes slower growth. It does not, however, protect against a shrinking margin: if earnings grow less than 8% a year and the market pays 15 times for them, the return is about zero.

One more number helps compare Netflix with other investments. Free cash flow excluding the termination fee and after stock-based compensation amounts to about 3.2% of market value: that is what an owner would receive each year if the company paid out all its cash and stopped growing.

## What to watch to know who is right

All four points are different views of the same question: can profit keep growing faster than viewing?

1. **The gap between revenue growth and content cost growth.** About three points this year. If it closes, the margin stops widening.
2. **Revenue growth in the US and Canada.** +11% in the third quarter, while the fourth quarter is compared with last year's 18%. Growth above 10% would support the comparison-base explanation.
3. **Content investment relative to the expense in the accounts.** The ratio was 1.17 over nine months. A higher ratio without faster growth means that keeping viewers is getting more expensive.
4. **Viewing hours and the first content under the new chief.** Netflix's next viewing report comes in early 2027, while series and films commissioned by Peter Rice will take a year or two.

The next milestone is the fourth-quarter results in January 2027, together with the first forecast for 2027.

## A conclusion everyone has to draw for themselves

The third quarter confirmed both stories at once. The business works: revenue is growing at double digits in every region, the margin is still widening on a comparable basis, and buybacks are at a record. At the same time, the company itself has admitted that **viewing is not growing as fast as the money**, and free cash flow excluding the termination fee is barely growing this year.

Anyone who thinks slow viewing is noise today pays about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings for a business with a 38% return on capital. Anyone who thinks it is an early sign sees $25 billion of content committed in advance and a margin that is widening by only a little over half a point a year. Who is right will be shown by the gap between revenue growth and content cost growth, the very number through which Netflix doubled its profit over the past four years.

Margin and operating income growth "excluding the Brazilian charge" are Engineering Investor calculations, not company figures. The 2026 content share in the chart is calculated from the company's forecast. Nielsen's shares apply to TV screens in the US and do not cover viewing on phones or in other countries. The return scenarios illustrate the arithmetic and are not a forecast.
