Netflix reported its third-quarter results on 20 October, after the US market closed: revenue was in line with its forecast and profit above it, but the forecast for the fourth quarter fell short of analysts' expectations and came with less advertising revenue. In after-hours trading the stock lost about 6%. This analysis looks at what the results confirm, what they change and what the price already includes.

## What the third quarter brought

| Third quarter | A year earlier | Company forecast (16 Jul) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $11.51bn | $12.86bn | $12.87bn |
| Revenue growth | +17.2% | +11.7% | +11.8% |
| Operating margin | 28.2% | 33.2% | **34.6%** |
| Earnings per share | $0.59 | $0.82 | **$0.87** |
| Free cash flow | $2.66bn | no forecast | $2.88bn |

**Operating income** is profit from the business itself: revenue minus all operating costs, before interest and taxes. **Operating margin** shows what share of revenue remains as operating income.

The company attributes the higher margin mainly to the timing of expenses. Operating income grew 37%, but last year's third quarter was reduced by a one-off charge of $619 million for a tax dispute in Brazil. Without it, growth is **15%**, and the margin is one point higher (33.6% last year, 34.6% this year).

The forecast is weaker than expected:

| Company forecast of 20 Oct | Forecast | For comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 revenue | $13.44bn (+11.5%) | analysts: $13.51bn |
| Q4 operating margin | 26.0% | last year: 24.5% |
| Q4 earnings per share | $0.67 | last year: $0.56 |
| 2026 revenue | $51.1bn | forecast of 16 Jul: $51.0bn to $51.4bn |
| 2026 advertising revenue | about $2.7bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $3bn |
| 2026 free cash flow | about $12.0bn | forecast of 16 Jul: about $12.5bn |

The fourth-quarter margin includes 1.2 points of layoff costs (27.2% without them); the full-year margin stays at 31.5%. According to the company, subscriptions are doing better than expected and advertising worse, because it is rolling out some advertising capabilities more slowly.

On 14 October, management announced it would lay off about 6% of staff, the first time since 2022. A day later, Netflix announced that **Peter Rice**, formerly president of Disney's television business, would become content chief on 26 October.

## The price shows when the market changed its mind

![Netflix: share price over the last 12 months](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/cena-12-mesecev.svg)

The last close before the results, **$68.69** (20 Oct 2026), is **44.7% below the 12-month peak** and 26.7% below the price at the start of the year. After the results, the stock fell to about $64.5 in after-hours trading. The biggest drops of the past year came the day after quarterly results:

| Day | Change | What was announced the evening before |
|---|---|---|
| 22 Oct 2025 | −10.1% | Q3 2025 results: profit lower because of the Brazilian tax charge |
| 17 Apr 2026 | −9.7% | Q1 2026 results above forecast, a forecast of slower growth; co-founder Reed Hastings leaves the board |
| 17 Jul 2026 | −7.3% | Q2 2026 results in line with forecast, a forecast of even slower growth; viewing data to be published less often |
| 21 Oct 2026 | about −6% after hours | Q3 2026 results above forecast, Q4 forecast below expectations, less advertising |

The pattern has now repeated for the third time this year: the past quarter matches or beats the forecast, and the stock falls on what the company says about the next one.

## How Netflix makes money: one cost spread over ever more memberships

Netflix sells a monthly subscription to series, films, live events and games, increasingly with advertising; at the end of 2025 it had more than 325 million paid memberships. The whole business rests on one property: **a series costs the same whether a hundred million households watch it or three hundred million.** The cost of content is fixed in advance, while revenue grows with every new membership and every price increase. In the reports this cost is called **content amortization**, because Netflix spreads the payment for a film or series over the years in which the content is watched.

![Content cost takes a falling share of revenue, operating margin is rising](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/vsebine-in-marza.svg)

| Item | 2021 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $29.7bn | $45.2bn | +52% |
| Content cost | $12.2bn | $16.4bn | +34% |
| Operating income | $6.2bn | $13.3bn | **+115%** |
| Content cost as a share of revenue | 41.2% | 36.3% | −4.8 points |
| Operating margin | 20.9% | 29.5% | +8.6 points |

Content cost grew more slowly than revenue, so of every additional dollar of revenue, 46 cents reached operating income. The mechanism also works in reverse: in 2022 content cost grew 14.7% and revenue only 6.5%, and the margin fell to 17.8%.

According to the company, revenue growth has three sources: new memberships, higher prices (at the end of March 2026 it raised the price of all plans in the US) and advertising (about $2.7 billion this year, a little over 5% of revenue).

![Most growth comes from outside the US and Canada](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/regije-delezi.svg)

The US and Canada bring in 44% of revenue and 41% of its growth. There, growth accelerated from 10% to 11% in the third quarter, the first with the full effect of the March price increase.

## The gap that margin comes from is narrowing

![The gap that margin comes from is narrowing this year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/skarje-rasti.svg)

Margin widens only as long as revenue grows faster than content cost. In 2024 and 2025 the gap was about eight percentage points; this year the company expects revenue growth of 13% and content cost growth of about 10%: **the gap narrows to about three points.**

| First nine months | 2025 | 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $33.13bn | $37.68bn | +13.7% |
| Content cost | $11.66bn | $12.76bn | +9.5% |
| Operating income excluding the Brazilian charge | $10.99bn | $12.61bn | +14.7% |
| Operating margin excluding the Brazilian charge | 33.2% | 33.5% | +0.3 points |
| Investment in new content | $12.04bn | $14.93bn | **+24%** |

On a comparable basis, the margin widened by only a third of a point over nine months. Netflix invested considerably more in new content than it expensed; the difference will reach the expense line in the coming years. Revenue growth is still falling, but ever more slowly: 11.8% in the third quarter, and the company forecasts 11.5% for the fourth.

For the full year, the company forecasts operating income growth of more than 20%, but last year's base was reduced by the Brazilian charge:

| Year 2026 | As the company forecasts | Excluding the Brazilian charge from last year's base |
|---|---|---|
| Operating margin 2025 | 29.5% | 30.9% |
| Operating margin 2026 | 31.5% | 31.5% |
| Margin expansion | 2.0 points | **0.6 points** |
| Operating income growth | more than 20% | **about 15%** |

The right-hand column is an Engineering Investor calculation. The mechanism still works, but with a smaller cushion than in the past two years: if revenue growth slips below content cost growth, the margin stops widening. Whether the slowdown is the start of permanently weaker growth or merely a comparison with an exceptionally strong year is the question on which the bear and bull cases part ways.

## A termination fee that distorts profit in both directions

On 5 December 2025, Netflix agreed to buy the film and streaming business of Warner Bros. Discovery. When Paramount Skydance offered more for the whole company, Netflix did not raise its offer; the agreement was terminated on 27 February 2026 and Netflix received a **termination fee of $2.8 billion**. The fee does not change operating income, but it raises net income: Netflix had forecast earnings per share of $0.76 for the first quarter and reported $1.23; the difference, **$0.47**, is almost entirely explained by the fee.

| Price/earnings ratio at a price of $68.69 | As reported by the company | Excluding the fee |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings for the last 12 months | 19.9 ($3.46) | **23.0** ($2.99) |
| 2026 earnings (nine months and the Q4 forecast) | 19.2 ($3.57) | **22.2** ($3.10) |
| Analyst estimate for 2027 | 18.1 ($3.80) | 18.1 ($3.80) |

Past profit therefore looks higher and future growth lower: estimated 2027 earnings per share are about 6% above this year's as reported by the company, and **about 23%** above this year's excluding the fee. The estimate dates from before the results; by how much analysts will revise it is not known today.

## The cash that actually remains

![This year more goes into buybacks than there is free cash](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/denar-in-odkupi.svg)

**Free cash flow** is cash from operations minus investment in equipment. Almost all of it goes into **share buybacks**: the company buys its own shares and retires them, so each remaining share represents a larger part of the company.

| Item | 2024 | 2025 | First nine months of 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free cash flow | $6.92bn | $9.46bn | $9.50bn |
| Share buybacks | $6.26bn | $9.13bn | **$10.78bn** |

This year Netflix has spent more on buybacks than it generated in free cash and covered the difference from its cash. In the third quarter it bought back 63.9 million shares at an average price of about $75 (Engineering Investor calculation); anyone buying at the last close pays 8.5% less than the company did in the third quarter. Authorised buybacks of $22.3 billion remain, 7.7% of market value. Market value is 24 times the free cash flow the company forecasts for this year, and the forecast includes the termination fee and a one-off real estate transaction. Stock-based compensation, which free cash flow does not deduct, amounts to about 4% of free cash flow.

Three numbers for confirmation:

- **Return on invested capital (ROIC)**, the after-tax profit on every dollar of equity and borrowed capital in the business, is about **38%**. The better-known return on equity (ROE) is less useful here, because buybacks and the termination fee inflate it artificially.
- **Debt is small:** net debt of $7.1 billion is a little under half of one year's operating income.
- **Content is committed in advance:** contractual content obligations amount to **$25.2 billion**, half of annual revenue, and have to be paid regardless of the number of members.

## The bear case: viewers are going elsewhere

Netflix competes for viewers' free time with everyone at once; among its rivals it lists Alphabet (owner of YouTube) and TikTok. The strongest evidence for the bear case is in viewing measurements.

![Netflix is second in the US, but lost a point in a year](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/gledanost-zda.svg)

| Share of TV time in the US (Nielsen) | July 2025 | July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | 13.4% | 14.2% (record) | +0.8 points |
| Netflix | 8.8% | **7.8%** | **−1.0 points** |

Netflix's own data points the same way: viewing hours grew only 2% in the first half of 2026, while memberships grew faster. In 2024 the company cited about two hours of viewing per membership per day; in the first half of 2026 it was about 1.6 (Engineering Investor calculation). In its third-quarter letter the company wrote for the first time that it is aware of investors' concern about growth in viewing hours and wants to accelerate it. In September, Wells Fargo built a rating equivalent to "sell" on this concern, with a price target of $57.

The logic of the case runs through the same mechanism as above, only in reverse:

1. Less viewing means a membership is worth less to a household.
2. A membership worth less is harder to raise in price and easier to cancel.
3. Revenue growth slows, while content cost is fixed in advance.
4. A fix costs money: more original content, more licensed content and sport, or an acquisition.

The outcome would be a repeat of 2022. Further support for the bears: a forecast below expectations, less advertising and less data, since Netflix no longer publishes membership numbers and from 2027 the viewing report will come out once a year. The layoffs and the new content chief can also be read as an admission that content has not worked as it should. And most content on YouTube is created by users themselves, so YouTube does not carry the risk that a title will fail; Netflix carries it in full.

## The bull case: the frightening numbers have another explanation

**Members are not leaving.** According to the company, the share of members who cancel improved in the third quarter globally and in the US, and revenue growth in the US and Canada accelerated after the price increase. People watching Netflix less because it was worth less to them would cancel more often at a higher price.

**Hours per membership fell because there are more memberships.** When Netflix began charging for password sharing in May 2023, people who had watched on someone else's account opened their own: there were 260 million memberships at the end of 2023 and 302 million at the end of 2024. Their viewing had already been counted in total hours, so the same number of hours was spread across more memberships.

![Many more memberships, only slightly more viewing hours](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/narocnine-in-ure.svg)

**Nielsen measures one screen in one country.** Netflix generates 56% of its revenue and 59% of its growth outside the US and Canada. In India, with a strong slate of local content, viewing hours grew 24% in the third quarter and revenue excluding currency effects 18%.

**Not all hours are worth the same.** Live events account for about 1% of viewing hours, yet six of the ten days with the most new members in the past five years were linked to them.

**The slowdown is mainly the comparison base.** Growth over the past two years was driven by paid password sharing in 2024 and the price increases at the start of 2025, after which growth in the US and Canada jumped to 18%. This year Netflix is being compared with exactly those quarters.

![US and Canada: growth is slightly higher again after the price increase](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/rast-zda-kanada.svg)

**Rivals are indebted, management is acting.** Paramount issued $52 billion of debt to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery, and according to the Financial Times the combined company will have about $80 billion of debt. Such a rival will find it harder to increase content spending and has a reason to sell its titles everywhere; Disney has already started licensing its films and series to Netflix. According to the company, the layoffs free up money to invest where it matters most, and to bulls the new content chief is a sign that management is solving the shortage of hits within the company, not through an acquisition.

| Analysts | Value | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Analysts with a price target | **32**, consensus "buy" | Most remain on the bull side |
| Average price target | $90.69 | +32% from the close of 20 Oct |
| Range of targets | from **$57** to **$125** | Disagreement by a factor of 2.2 |

The targets date from before the results; five banks had already lowered them in October.

## What the price already includes

![What has to hold for a purchase at this price to work out](/images/articles/netflix-analysis-2026-10-21/scenariji-donosa.svg)

A more useful question than whether the stock is "cheap" is **what Netflix has to achieve for a purchase at $68.69 to deliver a decent return.** The starting point is this year's earnings per share excluding the termination fee, about $3.10. The table shows the annual return over the next five years for different earnings growth rates and the price/earnings ratio the market would pay in five years:

- **8% growth and a ratio of 15** → about 0% a year.
- **12% growth and a ratio of 20** → 9.7% a year.
- **16% growth and a ratio of 25** → 18.8% a year.

Over the past two years, earnings per share grew faster than any row in the table (+65% and +28%), and even 10% revenue growth at an unchanged margin, together with buybacks, would mean earnings per share growth of about 13 to 14%. Over one year the market has sharply cut the multiple: according to Deutsche Bank, in June 2025 the stock traded at about 40 times expected earnings, and today at about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings. The price therefore already assumes slower growth, but it does not protect against a shrinking margin: if earnings grow less than 8% a year and the market pays 15 times for them, the return is zero. If the price settles at its level after the results, all the ratios in this analysis are about 6% lower.

## What to watch to know who is right

1. **The gap between revenue growth and content cost growth.** About three points this year. If it closes, the margin stops widening.
2. **Revenue growth in the US and Canada.** +11% in the third quarter. The fourth quarter is compared with last year's 18%; growth above 10% would support the comparison-base explanation.
3. **Content investment relative to the expense in the accounts.** The ratio was 1.17 over nine months. A higher ratio without faster growth means that keeping viewers is getting more expensive.
4. **Viewing hours and the first content under the new chief.** Netflix's next viewing report comes in early 2027, while series and films commissioned by Peter Rice will take a year or two.

The next milestone is the fourth-quarter results in January 2027, together with the first forecast for 2027.

## A conclusion everyone has to draw for themselves

The third quarter confirmed both stories at once. The business works: revenue is growing at double digits in every region, the margin is still widening on a comparable basis, and buybacks are at a record. And the question that pushed the stock 45% below its peak has now been put in writing by the company itself: **viewing is not growing as fast as the money.**

Anyone who thinks this is noise today pays about 18 times estimated 2027 earnings for a business with a 38% return on capital. Anyone who thinks it is an early sign sees $25 billion of content committed in advance and a margin that is widening by only a little over half a point a year on a comparable basis. The answer will come from the gap between revenue growth and content cost growth in the coming years.
