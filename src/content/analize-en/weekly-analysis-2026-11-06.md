## Themes of the week

### 1. Democrats win Congress, the Dow has its best day in a year and a half

The midterm elections on Tuesday, 3 November, were the main event of the week. Before the week began, Bloomberg wrote that traders were worried about what a Democratic win would mean for the build-out of data centres for artificial intelligence, while Morgan Stanley strategist Mike Wilson advised buying stocks regardless of the outcome.

Democrats won majorities in both the House of Representatives and the Senate. When the first results on Tuesday evening New York time pointed to their victory, Nasdaq 100 futures fell to their lowest level since July, according to MarketWatch, and overnight into Wednesday the dollar weakened the most in two months, according to Bloomberg. On Wednesday morning futures turned higher, and JPMorgan strategists, according to MarketWatch, called the election a clearing event that removes uncertainty.

The Dow Jones rose 3.03% on Wednesday, its best day since April 2025 according to CNBC. The S&P 500 gained 1.58% on Wednesday, and the consumer discretionary sector, which includes retailers and carmakers, rose 4.26%. MarketWatch attributed the rally to hopes that a Democratic-led Congress would hasten the end of the war with Iran; oil fell almost 9% the same day (theme 3).

Elon Musk's companies were among the losers. According to CNBC, Tesla and SpaceX fell about 4% before the open on Wednesday after candidates backed by Musk lost in Texas (New York Times). Chipmakers, including Micron, also fell: according to MarketWatch, Democratic wins in Texas and other states called the data-centre build-out into question. That was the prelude to Thursday's slump (theme 2).

### 2. The worst day for tech stocks since 2020

The tech sell-off that began in October deepened this week. On Monday the Nasdaq 100, the index of the hundred largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq exchange, fell 1.39% and, according to MarketWatch, entered its third correction this year, meaning it was more than 10% below its peak.

Thursday brought the crash: the Nasdaq 100 fell 6.36% on Thursday, the S&P 500 4.36%, the Dow Jones 3.44% or 1,833 points, and the information technology sector 8%. According to MarketWatch it was the worst day for tech stocks since 2020, and the S&P 500 lost about $2.9 trillion in market value, the most ever in a single day. The VIX index, which measures expected stock volatility and is also called the fear index, rose from 16.61 to 28.18 points.

The Financial Times attributed the fall to investors' fear that newly elected politicians would try to slow the construction of data centres. According to Bloomberg, the selling was amplified by funds that automatically follow price trends. Shares of companies linked to artificial intelligence and data centres fell sharply, including Nvidia, Sandisk and CoreWeave (Yahoo Finance), and Qualcomm lost 9% on Thursday after its results (CNBC).

Asian markets fell twice. On Thursday, before the crash in New York, South Korea's Kospi lost more than 5%, according to Yahoo Finance, and trading was briefly halted. On Friday the Kospi fell 8%, according to CNBC, and below 5,000 points, according to Bloomberg, while Japan's Nikkei 225 and Topix indices lost almost 6%, according to the Financial Times, their biggest one-day fall since April 2025.

US tech stocks recovered somewhat on Friday: the Nasdaq 100 gained 1.35% and the S&P 500 0.31%, while the Dow Jones lost 0.46%. The Financial Times called the recovery tepid. Over the week the S&P 500 lost 2.68%, the Nasdaq 100 4.91% and the information technology sector 8.23%. The communication services sector, which includes Alphabet and Meta, gained 4.89% over the week: it rose 3.47% on Wednesday and lost only 0.59% on Thursday.

### 3. Oil has its biggest weekly fall since April, gold rises

Brent, the European benchmark crude, fell 13.32% over the week to $98.32 a barrel. The change is calculated on the January contract; last week the table showed the December one. Bloomberg on Friday called it the biggest weekly fall since April, and the energy sector of the S&P 500 lost 12% over the week.

On Monday oil fell because the US and Iran were seeking a diplomatic solution, according to CNBC. US WTI crude dropped below $100 before the stock market opened, and Brent ended the day 7.6% lower. The same evening, according to CNBC, the US military struck Iran's Qeshm Island. Oil rose on Tuesday morning, with the Financial Times reporting a price above $106, but Brent ended the day 1.1% lower.

On Wednesday, the day after the election, Brent lost 8.67%. According to the Financial Times, Vice President JD Vance blamed the Republicans' defeat on voters' anger over the war with Iran, and according to Bloomberg investors bet that the Democratic win would hasten the end of the war. On Thursday Brent rose 3.88%, and on Friday it was almost unchanged.

Gold rose 4.51% over the week to $4,074.90 an ounce, according to Bloomberg the most since August. It gained 1.81% on Monday because hopes of peace lowered the oil price and with it the fear of inflation, and 3.37% on Wednesday because the dollar and Treasury yields fell after the election (Bloomberg). Bitcoin lost 1.88% over the week.

### 4. A stronger-than-expected labour market, bets on a rate hike

US labour market data beat expectations all week. On Tuesday the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported 7.5 million job openings in September. Bloomberg and CNBC reported a rise of 333,000, well above the expected 7.15 million, while the BLS describes the change as little changed. On Wednesday the company ADP said private employers added 86,000 jobs in October, slightly more than expected.

On Friday the BLS reported that 165,000 nonfarm jobs were created in October, while analysts had expected 59,000, according to CNBC. Unemployment fell to 4.1% (4.2% in September), and average hourly earnings were 3.1% higher than a year earlier. The BLS revised the September figure for new jobs from 29,000 to 60,000.

The yield on the two-year Treasury, which tracks expectations for Fed interest rates most closely, rose 5 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point) to 4.51% on Friday. Bloomberg wrote that the data backed bets that the Fed would raise interest rates as early as December, and San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly said further hikes would likely be needed. Over the week the two-year yield still fell 5 basis points, because investors bought bonds during Thursday's stock sell-off: that day it fell 12 basis points. The ten-year yield fell 8 basis points over the week to 4.86%.

### 5. The Bank of England and Australia's central bank raise rates

While markets bet on a December hike in the US, two other central banks raised rates this week. The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its key rate on Tuesday to its highest level since 2008, its fifth increase this year, and the decision was not unanimous (Bloomberg, Financial Times).

The Bank of England raised its rate to 4% on Thursday, the first increase since 2023. All members of the committee voted for a hike, one of them for a bigger one, to 4.25% (CNBC, Bloomberg). The bank expects inflation in the UK to approach 5% next year (New York Times), and Governor Andrew Bailey said another hike was more likely than not if the high energy costs of recent weeks returned (Bloomberg). According to Bloomberg, hopes of an end to the war with Iran after the US election came too late to prevent the hike. Before the decision, Bloomberg's gauge of pound strength rose to its highest level since the Brexit referendum in 2016.
